Chapter 1: Getting started — installation and the workspace

1.1 What is AquaProv?

AquaProv is a Python desktop application built with PySide6 and Matplotlib. Import a laboratory table, explore it across seven modules, and preserve the dataset and analysis settings in a portable .aquaprov project. Numerical results and methods can be exported alongside each supported analytical figure.

1.2 Motivation behind AquaProv

Groundwater studies often involve preparing the same laboratory table for hydrochemical diagrams, statistical analyses, maps and water-quality indices. Moving between separate spreadsheets, scripts and plotting tools makes it harder to keep sample selections, units and analysis settings consistent.

We developed AquaProv to bring these steps into one accessible desktop workspace for students, researchers and practising hydrogeologists. A shared dataset connects water chemistry, statistics, spatial patterns, water-quality assessment and mineral saturation. Users can explore these relationships through the graphical interface, while Python interfaces support scripted analyses.

A second motivation is to make the evidence behind a result easier to review. Measurements near a classification boundary or screening threshold can lead to different conclusions when uncertainty or analysis choices change. AquaProv provides measurement-quality checks and uncertainty tools, and keeps available numerical results, settings and method details alongside figures. Saved projects help users revisit their work and report how a result was produced.

AquaProv visualizes and analyzes supplied measurements. Hydrochemical facies, correlations, and clusters support interpretation; they do not independently identify contamination sources, demonstrate potability, or replace a hydrogeological conceptual model. The current Assessment module includes a limited, versioned chemical screening profile, described separately from geochemical diagrams.

Chapter 2: Preparing and importing water-chemistry data

The quality of an analysis begins with the meaning of the measurements. A recognizable column heading does not establish a unit, a reporting basis or the independence of a sample. This chapter explains how to move a laboratory table into AquaProv while retaining those distinctions. The Sukinda workbook is used for the first import because the same 47 records support the chemistry, statistics and mapping examples later in the manual.

2.1 Measurement meaning, chemical units and charge balance

Major-ion diagrams compare ionic charge contributions, whereas laboratory reports commonly give mass concentrations. For an ion with concentration C in mg/L, molar mass M in g/mol and absolute charge |z|, its millimolar concentration is C/M and its equivalent concentration is |z|C/M. The numerical equivalence between g/mol and mg/mmol makes these expressions directly usable. Piper-type normalization must follow this conversion; mass percentages do not represent equivalent percentages (Piper, 1944).

mi = Ci/Mi (mmol/L);   ei = |zi|Ci/Mi (meq/L) (2.1)

Illustrative calculation. Calcium at 40.078 mg/L corresponds to 1 mmol/L and 2 meq/L; sodium at 22.9898 mg/L corresponds to 1 mmol/L and 1 meq/L. Equal molar concentrations therefore make different contributions to charge. Record the chemical reporting basis as well as the unit: nitrate expressed as nitrogen requires a species conversion, and an alkalinity result expressed as CaCO3 is not a measured calcium concentration.

The assessment charge-balance diagnostic uses CBE = 100(Tc − Ta)/(Tc + Ta), where Tc and Ta are measured cation and anion totals in meq/L. For totals of 5 and 4 meq/L, the illustrative CBE is +11.11%. AquaProv's default absolute tolerance of 5% is a review setting, not proof of analytical acceptability. The core calculation requires all eight mapped major ions; a separate extended diagnostic includes nitrate and fluoride when both are measured. Missing carbonate is not silently replaced with zero. A small imbalance can conceal compensating errors and should be read alongside laboratory quality information.

2.2 Sampling design and the evidence supplied with the examples

A sample identifier should distinguish a sampling event from the identity of a well. Repeated campaigns from the same well support temporal comparisons, but they do not automatically increase the number of independent spatial locations. This distinction matters in cluster interpretation and campaign comparisons, as illustrated by the regional and repeated-observation studies of Yang et al. (2020) and Liu et al. (2021). Keep dates, sites and grouping columns available even when they are excluded from the numeric matrix.

The Sukinda workbook filename identifies a 2024 dataset; its associated aquifer-management report is cited as Sahoo et al. (2025). Report year and sampling year serve different purposes and should not be substituted for one another. The other worked datasets are attributed to Moreno Merino et al. (2021), Liu et al. (2021) and Yang et al. (2020). Cite those sources for their observations, and describe the actual worksheet, campaign or subset used in the AquaProv analysis.

2.7 Select variables deliberately

Log-ratio analysis treats relative composition through ratios rather than independent raw percentages (Aitchison, 1982; Egozcue et al., 2003). For statistics, review IDs, dates, coordinate columns, constant variables, and redundant totals before including them. Do not analyze Ca, Mg, and hardness as independent chemical species without considering their relationship. Do not include both Na and a Na+K total as if they were unrelated components.

Major-ion proportions sum to a fixed total. Correlation and PCA on such compositions can behave differently from analysis of absolute concentrations. Use compositional PCA when log-ratio analysis matches your question.

Chapter 3: Groundwater and contamination diagrams

The Groundwater & contamination module contains 14 diagram types. Several describe the proportions of major ions, whereas others retain concentration magnitude or combine a chemical ratio with salinity. A point at a similar location on two different diagrams does not necessarily represent the same quantity. Choose the diagram according to the question, then review its required ions and unit convention.

3.1 Equivalent fractions and the limits of facies classification

Piper and related diagrams reduce the major-ion composition to separately normalized cation and anion groups (Piper, 1944). Let e denote equivalent concentration. AquaProv combines sodium with potassium and bicarbonate with carbonate, and requires positive cation and anion totals before forming fractions:

Tc = eCa + eMg + eNa + eK
Ta = eHCO3 + eCO3 + eCl + eSO4
c = (eCa, eMg, eNa + eK)/Tc
a = (eHCO3 + eCO3, eSO4, eCl)/Ta (3.1)

Each triplet sums to one; multiplying by 100 gives equivalent percentages. Multiplying every concentration in a sample by the same positive factor leaves these fractions unchanged. Consequently, a facies position describes relative composition and does not by itself establish dissolved-solute concentration, charge balance or a unique reaction pathway. Use measured TDS or EC, the analytical audit and independent geological evidence to develop a process interpretation (Güler et al., 2002).

3.4 Triangle Piper

Scientific basis. Piper (1944) introduced the trilinear framework to compare cation and anion proportions and combine them in a central diamond. Each triangle is a weighted average of three endmember vertices; the diamond is a projection of those two points, not an additional measurement. The Ca–Mg–(Na+K) and (HCO3+CO3)–SO4–Cl groups must therefore use the same equivalent-concentration convention throughout a study.

For example, a Ca fraction of 0.50 denotes half of the measured cation equivalents, not half of all dissolved material. Interpret a cluster of nearby points as similar relative major-ion composition. Mixing, exchange and mineral reactions can produce overlapping trajectories, so process names should remain hypotheses until checked against hydrogeology and additional chemical evidence.

Purpose.

Compare major-ion composition across samples using two ternary plots and a central diamond.

How it works.

The cation triangle uses Ca, Mg, and Na + K as percentages of total cation equivalents. The anion triangle uses HCO3 + CO3, SO4, and Cl as percentages of total anion equivalents. Projection into the diamond combines those proportions.

Ion percentage = 100 × ion-group meq/L / total cation or anion meq/L

Reference: Piper (1944).

3.5 Rectangle Piper

Scientific basis. The rectangular representation reorganizes Piper proportions into aligned coordinate panels (Ray & Mukherjee, 2008). AquaProv plots Ca versus Mg percentages, Cl versus SO4 percentages, and combined alkalis versus strong-acid anions. The complementary totals follow from the same separate cation and anion normalization used in the triangle diagram.

Read the axis labels and direction before comparing points between panels. A reversal of an axis changes the visual direction of a trend without changing the underlying chemistry. The rectangular view can make comparisons easier to follow, but it retains the relative-composition limitations of the original framework.

Purpose.

Compare Piper-style major-ion composition in rectangular coordinates.

How it works.

A rectangular transformation displays the equivalent-percent chemistry in aligned panels. The transformation changes the graphical presentation, while the same verified ion totals and units remain essential.

Equivalent proportions are computed separately for cations and anions.

Distinguish the background from sample colours assigned to groups in the legend. Neither background intensity nor hue is a direct measurement of contamination, sample density or water quality. A figure caption should state whether a colour represents the composition scheme, a selected measurement or a categorical sample group.

3.7 Contour-filled Piper

Scientific basis. Dense datasets can obscure structure through overlapping symbols; density displays provide an alternative view of sample concentration within Piper space (Russoniello & Lautz, 2020; Moreno Merino et al., 2021). AquaProv's contour-filled implementation fits a two-dimensional Gaussian kernel-density estimate separately to the two triangles and the diamond, with bandwidth 0.05 in diagram coordinates and a 500 × 500 evaluation grid.

These are AquaProv display choices, not a claim to reproduce every published large-dataset method. All retained rows contribute equally, so repeated observations can intensify a density peak. Masking at panel boundaries does not correct the statistical boundary bias. Read contours as density of observations in composition space, and retain the ordinary point plot to inspect sample support.

Purpose.

Reveal dense regions in Piper composition space when point clouds overlap.

How it works.

The implementation estimates point density in transformed Piper space and displays filled contours. Higher density indicates a greater local concentration of sample points in composition space.

Density describes the distribution of plotted compositions.

Reference: Russoniello and Lautz (2020).

3.8 Durov

Scientific basis. Durov's graphical approach projects normalized major-ion groups into a central square (Durov, 1948). AquaProv's extended display adds measured pH and TDS, placing relative composition alongside acidity and salinity. The central square still depends on equivalent fractions; pH and TDS are separate observations.

Check the input units even when a plotted axis rescales TDS for presentation. Two waters may share a central-square position while differing greatly in TDS. Use the auxiliary panels to identify such differences, and avoid assigning mixing or exchange mechanisms solely from the square position.

Purpose.

Read cation–anion composition with accompanying pH and total dissolved solids.

How it works.

Two normalized major-ion triangles project into a central composition field. Auxiliary pH and TDS axes place the composition alongside measured acidity and dissolved-solids concentration.

Ion input: mg/L or meq/L · TDS: mg/L · pH: unitless

3.18 Mathematical background of the plots

The following sections explain how the inputs become plotted coordinates, summaries or predictions. Each section names the views that use it. Shared formulas are stated once within this chapter; follow the applicable preprocessing and calculation sections together. Examples labelled illustrative are arithmetic checks, not findings from the field datasets.

3.18.1 From laboratory concentrations to chemical equivalents

Applies to: all plots in this chapter.

A mass concentration gives the mass of an ion; an equivalent concentration also accounts for its electric charge. Let Cᵢ be concentration in mg/L, Mᵢ molar mass in g/mol (numerically also mg/mmol), and |zᵢ| the absolute ionic charge (Piper, 1944):

mᵢ = Cᵢ / Mᵢ (mmol/L)
eᵢ = |zᵢ| Cᵢ / Mᵢ (meq/L) (3.4)

For an input already in meq/L, eᵢ is the supplied value. To recover mmol/L from meq/L, divide by |zᵢ|. These conversions are different for divalent ions such as Ca²⁺ and monovalent ions such as Na⁺.

Ion Mᵢ (g/mol) Absolute charge
Ca 40.0780 2
Mg 24.3050 2
Na 22.9898 1
K 39.0983 1
HCO₃ 61.0171 1
CO₃ 60.0092 2
Cl 35.4527 1
SO₄ 96.0636 2

Illustrative check: 40.078 mg/L Ca is 1 mmol/L and 2 meq/L; 22.9898 mg/L Na is 1 mmol/L and 1 meq/L. Equal molar concentrations do not mean equal charge contributions. Concentrations must be finite and nonnegative. A missing measurement does not become a measured zero; check the selected missing-data policy and any explicitly supplied values.

3.18.2 Normalize cations and anions separately

Applies to: Triangle Piper; Rectangle Piper; Color-coded Piper; Contour-filled Piper; Durov; Chadha; Hydrochemical facies evolution diagram (HFE-D).

Using equivalents eᵢ, form the two charge totals and the six grouped fractions (Piper, 1944):

Tc = eCa + eMg + eNa + eK
Ta = eHCO₃ + eCO₃ + eCl + eSO₄
c = (eCa, eMg, eNa + eK) / Tc
a = (eHCO₃ + eCO₃, eSO₄, eCl) / Ta (3.5)

Below, c₁, c₂, c₃ mean Ca, Mg and Na+K fractions, and a₁, a₂, a₃ mean HCO₃+CO₃, SO₄ and Cl fractions. Each triplet sums to 1; multiply by 100 for equivalent percentages. Both totals must be positive. AquaProv checks this before constructing normalized major-ion diagrams.

Normalization explains why these diagrams describe relative composition. Multiplying every concentration in a sample by ten leaves its position unchanged. Separate normalization also hides disagreement between cation and anion totals, so a plausible plotted position does not establish good charge balance.

3.18.3 Piper geometry: two triangles and a projected diamond

Applies to: Triangle Piper; Color-coded Piper; Contour-filled Piper.

A ternary point is a weighted average of three vertices. With triangle height h = √3/2 and the diagram's horizontal gap o = 0.1, AquaProv uses (Piper, 1944):

xc = c₃ + c₂/2;   yc = h c₂
xa = 1 + 2o + a₃ + a₂/2;   ya = h a₂
xd = (xa + xc)/2 + (ya − yc)/(4h)
yd = (ya + yc)/2 + h(xa − xc) (3.6)

The cation vertices represent Ca (left), Na+K (right) and Mg (top); the anion vertices represent HCO₃+CO₃ (left), Cl (right) and SO₄ (top). The diamond combines the two compositions by parallel projection. Its location is not an extra chemical measurement.

Illustrative check: c = (0.5, 0.25, 0.25) and a = (0.5, 0.25, 0.25) give (xc, yc) = (0.375, 0.2165), (xa, ya) = (1.575, 0.2165), and (xd, yd) ≈ (0.975, 1.2557). The fractions, rather than the physical page coordinates, carry the chemical meaning.

Piper's framework compares major-ion facies. Similar points can result from different reaction or mixing histories; use salinity, charge balance and independent hydrogeological evidence alongside the diagram.

3.18.4 Rectangular Piper coordinates

Applies to: Rectangle Piper.

This rearranges the equivalent proportions into aligned rectangular panels. The cation panel plots Ca and Mg percentages, the anion panel plots Cl and SO₄ percentages, and the central panel combines alkalis with strong-acid anions (Ray & Mukherjee, 2008):

Cation panel: (100c₁, 100c₂)
Anion panel: (100a₃, 100a₂)
Combined panel: (100c₃, 100(a₂ + a₃)) (3.7)

Complementary axes show 100 − 100c₃ = Ca+Mg% and 100 − 100(a₂+a₃) = HCO₃+CO₃%. Some axes run in the reverse direction; read their labels. For the illustrative triplets (0.5, 0.25, 0.25), the combined coordinates are (25%, 50%). Moving the display into rectangles preserves the chemical normalization and does not recover absolute concentration.

3.18.5 How composition becomes background colour

Applies to: Color-coded Piper.

The points use the Piper coordinates above. A fixed, bundled colour-interpolation resource maps composition-space coordinates to red, green and blue channels. For example, the cation triangle first transforms its point as (Peeters, 2014):

u = 2xc − yc/h − 1
v = 2xc + yc/h − 1
RGBk = clip(Fk(u, v), 0, 1),   k ∈ {red, green, blue} (3.8)

Fₖ is the stored interpolator for channel k. The anion and diamond regions have their corresponding coordinate transforms into the same colour system. The function returns sample RGB arrays for all three regions. The background therefore encodes position, not the number of samples, a concentration or a risk score. A change in the sample legend's group colours has a different meaning from this method-defined background.

3.18.6 Gaussian kernel density in Piper space

Applies to: Contour-filled Piper.

AquaProv fits a separate two-dimensional Gaussian kernel density estimate to each triangle and the diamond. For points pᵢ and a location p in the same panel (Russoniello & Lautz, 2020; Moreno Merino et al., 2021):

f̂(p) = [1 / (n · 2πb²)] ∑i=1…n exp(−‖p − pᵢ‖² / (2b²))
b = 0.05 in diagram-coordinate units (3.9)

The code uses scikit-learn KernelDensity, evaluates a 500 × 500 support grid, and masks values outside the panel polygons. Filled levels follow the estimated density range. This is a display mask, not a boundary-corrected or renormalized density estimator, so kernels near a triangle edge still extend outside the domain mathematically.

All retained samples contribute equally; repeated observations can increase local density. Larger bandwidth would smooth more and smaller bandwidth would show finer structure; this plotting function fixes b at 0.05. The colours describe concentration of observations in composition space, not dissolved-solute concentration or geographic abundance. Compare panel patterns with their scales and the ordinary point plot.

3.18.7 Durov projection and auxiliary measurements

Applies to: Durov.

The major-ion fractions are projected from the two triangles into a square. Using the fractions defined above (Durov, 1948):

xc = −(√3/2)c₂;   yc = c₁ + c₂/2
xa = a₁ + a₂/2;   ya = 1 + (√3/2)a₂
Central point = (xa, yc) (3.10)

The extra panels retain measured TDS and pH. Internally the drawing converts TDS from mg/L to g/L, then uses xTDS = 1 + 1.618(TDSg/L/4). It maps pH to ypH = −0.618(pH − pHmin)/(pHmax − pHmin), where limits use the floor/ceiling of observed pH (expanded if equal). These are graphical placements; they do not alter the chemical measurements. TDS stays in mg/L at input even when ion units are meq/L. Missing auxiliary measurements cannot be reconstructed from the central square.

The extended view makes relative ion composition and absolute salinity visible together. A square position alone is not a unique diagnosis of mixing, ion exchange or dissolution.

3.18.8 A polygon of equivalent concentrations

Applies to: Stiff patterns.

Stiff patterns use the converted equivalents directly, without normalizing them to percentages. The three paired levels connect Na+K with Cl, Ca with HCO₃, and Mg with SO₄ (Stiff, 1951):

Left magnitudes: eNa + eK, eCa, eMg
Right magnitudes: eCl, eHCO₃, eSO₄ (3.11)

Cations are drawn with negative horizontal coordinates only to place them left of the origin; the measured concentrations are nonnegative. Carbonate is not included in the bicarbonate vertex in this implementation. One polygon is generated per sample. If all six magnitudes double, the polygon doubles in horizontal width at a fixed axis scale. Its area is not a defined TDS, charge-balance or water-quality index.

Illustrative check: Na+K = 2, Ca = 3, Mg = 1 meq/L and Cl = 2, HCO₃ = 3, SO₄ = 1 meq/L produce equal opposing widths. Compare axis limits across figures before comparing polygon sizes.

3.18.9 Ion fractions control facial geometry

Applies to: Chernoff faces.

This face uses cation fractions of Ca, Mg and Na+K, and anion fractions of HCO₃, Cl and SO₄. The anion denominator here is eHCO₃ + eCl + eSO₄; CO₃ is not included. Six chemistry fractions control visible geometry (Chernoff, 1973):

Feature Parameter used by the drawing
Upper face half-width Mg fraction + 0.25
Lower face half-width Ca fraction + 0.25
Nose length 0.3 × (Na+K fraction + 0.01)
Mouth curvature parameter k 5 × (SO₄ fraction + 0.001)
Mouth width parameter w HCO₃ fraction
Eye width Cl fraction + 0.1

The mouth is an arc with diameter 1/k and angular half-span arctan(kw); the other facial proportions are fixed drawing constants. Small offsets keep features visible at zero fractions. This is a deterministic visual encoding, not an inferred emotion or a statistical distance. Compare actual concentrations to understand a face difference; normalization removes absolute concentration information.

3.18.10 Logarithmic ion profiles

Applies to: Schoeller.

For each sample AquaProv connects equivalent concentrations in the order Ca, Mg, Na, K, Cl, SO₄, HCO₃. The vertical axis is logarithmic (Schoeller, 1962):

Vertical separation ∝ log₁₀(e₂) − log₁₀(e₁) = log₁₀(e₂/e₁) (3.12)

Thus the vertical gap between 0.1 and 1 meq/L equals the gap between 1 and 10 meq/L. Na and K are separate positions. Shape represents relative enrichment among ions, while vertical level retains concentration magnitude. Segments connect different chemical species; they are not time trends or reactions. A zero has no finite logarithm and cannot be interpreted as a small positive concentration. Check zero/missing entries and any warnings rather than replacing them invisibly.

3.18.11 Molar ratios against total dissolved solids

Applies to: Gibbs.

The Gibbs panels use mmol/L, not charge equivalents, for the ratios in this implementation (Gibbs, 1970; Marandi & Shand, 2018):

Rcation = mNa / (mNa + mCa)
Ranion = mCl / (mCl + mHCO₃) (3.13)

Ratios occupy the horizontal axes; independently supplied TDS in mg/L occupies logarithmic vertical axes. The denominators and TDS must be positive for interpretable points. K and CO₃ do not enter these ratios. Illustrative check: 22.9898 mg/L Na and 40.078 mg/L Ca give Rcation = 0.5 on a molar basis; using their equivalent concentrations would incorrectly give 1/3 for this implementation.

The reference envelopes describe broad precipitation, rock interaction and evaporation/crystallization tendencies from the original conceptual framework. They are not fitted to the imported dataset and do not uniquely classify groundwater processes. Mixing, anthropogenic inputs and local mineralogy can generate similar ratios.

3.18.12 Differences between equivalent-percentage groups

Applies to: Chadha.

Chadha's coordinates compare alkaline earths with alkalis and weak-acid with strong-acid anions (Chadha, 1999):

x = 100[(c₁ + c₂) − c₃] = 100(1 − 2c₃)
y = 100[a₁ − (a₂ + a₃)] = 100(2a₁ − 1) (3.14)

Both axes span −100 to +100 percentage points. x > 0 means Ca+Mg exceeds Na+K in equivalents; y > 0 means HCO₃+CO₃ exceeds Cl+SO₄. Zero is an equal split between the two groups, not absence of ions. For c₃ = 0.25 and a₁ = 0.5, the point is (50, 0). The quadrants summarize group dominance and proposed hydrochemical tendencies; concentrations and distinct mixtures can collapse to the same coordinates.

3.18.13 Sodium-normalized weathering ratios

Applies to: Gaillardet.

The two log–log panels use molar concentrations (Gaillardet et al., 1999):

x = mCa/mNa
y₁ = mHCO₃/mNa;   y₂ = mMg/mNa (3.15)

Convert mass to mmol/L before dividing; if input is meq/L, divide each ion by its absolute charge first. Positive Na and positive plotted ratios are required on log axes. A tenfold increase in a ratio produces a one-decade displacement. For mmol/L values Ca = 2, Na = 1, HCO₃ = 3 and Mg = 0.5, the two points are (2, 3) and (2, 0.5).

Carbonate, silicate and evaporite reference fields are conceptual comparisons, not a fitted end-member mixing model. A small Na denominator amplifies errors in every displayed ratio; the shared denominator can itself induce association. The large-river weathering framework needs independent support when applied to groundwater.

3.18.14 Piecewise HFE-D coordinates and reference mixing line

Applies to: Hydrochemical facies evolution diagram (HFE-D).

Let C = 100c, A = 100a, rc = max(C₁, C₂), and ra = max(A₁, A₂). The implementation switches branches according to the dominant competing group (Giménez-Forcada, 2010):

x = rc − 100/3 + 66.7 if rc > C₃; otherwise x = 100 − C₃
y = ra − 100/3 + 66.7 if ra > A₃; otherwise y = 100 − A₃ (3.16)

Here C₃ is Na+K%, A₃ is Cl%, and A₁ combines HCO₃+CO₃. The 66.7 constant is the drawing convention used in the source. These piecewise coordinates arrange the reference facies used to discuss salinization and freshening.

The reference line joins a fixed seawater composition to (max x, max y) from the plotted dataset. Those two maxima need not belong to the same observed sample. The seawater endpoint uses the bundled Ca, Mg, Na, K, HCO₃, Cl and SO₄ concentrations converted to equivalents. Consequently the line is a visual reference, not an estimated seawater mixing fraction or independently measured recharge end member. Temporal observations are needed to infer a direction of evolution.

3.18.15 SAR and conductivity-dependent USSL boundaries

Applies to: USSL irrigation classification.

Using Na, Ca and Mg in meq/L, AquaProv calculates sodium adsorption ratio (SAR) (US Salinity Laboratory Staff, 1954):

SAR = eNa / √[(eCa + eMg)/2] (3.17)

Ca+Mg must be positive. The horizontal axis is measured EC referenced to 25 °C, expressed in µS/cm; 1 mS/cm = 1 dS/m = 1,000 µS/cm. No TDS-to-EC estimate or temperature correction is performed. The salinity class changes at EC = 250, 750 and 2,250 µS/cm. Sodium-hazard boundaries are curves, with E denoting the numerical EC value in µS/cm:

S1/S2: SAR = 18.87 − 4.44 log₁₀E
S2/S3: SAR = 31.31 − 6.66 log₁₀E
S3/S4: SAR = 43.75 − 8.87 log₁₀E (3.18)

Each crossed boundary increases the sodium class; exact ties use the higher class. The combined reference domain is 100 ≤ EC ≤ 5,000 and 0 ≤ SAR ≤ 32. Values outside it are reported explicitly. Review flags mark points within 0.5 SAR of a sodium curve or 25 µS/cm of a salinity cut; these are review tolerances, not uncertainty intervals.

Illustrative check: Ca = 2, Mg = 2 and Na = 2 meq/L give SAR = 1.414. At EC = 1,000, the S1/S2 boundary is 5.55 and the point is C3–S1. Field suitability also depends on soil, crop, drainage and irrigation management.

Chapter 4: Statistical analysis

The Statistics module describes relationships among selected numeric variables and differences among samples. Its 13 views cover correlation, principal components, hierarchical clustering, distributions, observed time series, measured series in source-row order and bootstrap intervals. They share a data-selection step but answer different questions: a correlation heatmap compares variables, a score plot compares samples, and a loading plot explains how variables contribute to the components. Measured series retain original units and source-row positions without requiring dates.

4.1 Statistical questions, preprocessing and inferential limits

Statistical summaries are conditional on the rows and variables supplied to the analysis. Standardization gives variables comparable variance, whereas a centered analysis retains their original scale effects. This choice can alter the apparent importance of salinity-related variables and the separation of hydrochemical groups (Güler et al., 2002; Jolliffe & Cadima, 2016). AquaProv uses the retained cohort's sample standard deviation, with divisor n−1, for its z-scores. It does not infer a logarithmic transformation or replace a missing result automatically.

zij = (xij − meanj)/sj;   sj2 = Σi(xij − meanj)2/(n−1) (4.1)

For the illustrative values 1, 2 and 3, the mean is 2, the sample SD is 1 and the standardized values are −1, 0 and 1. A constant column has no usable standardized variance. Document any explicit exclusion or transformation and retain the final sample count; a figure generated from a reduced cohort describes that cohort.

4.1.1 Correlation and multiple testing

Pearson's coefficient describes linear association, while Spearman's coefficient describes association between ranks and can reveal a monotonic relationship without requiring linearity in the original values (Pearson, 1895; Spearman, 1904). Neither establishes a causal chemical mechanism. AquaProv uses one common complete-case cohort across the selected matrix, so comparisons do not silently use different row subsets.

With p selected variables there are p(p−1)/2 unique off-diagonal comparisons. AquaProv can apply the Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment over that family and display significance using adjusted q-values (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). Report which display was used and retain both coefficients and adjusted results. Repeated wells, spatial dependence and small samples can undermine the ordinary significance interpretation; multiplicity adjustment does not remove those design limitations.

4.1.2 Principal components and cluster structure

PCA identifies orthogonal directions of variation in the centered analysis matrix (Jolliffe & Cadima, 2016). If Z = UDVT, AquaProv computes component eigenvalues dk2/(n−1), scores ZV and variance-scaled loadings V diag(√λ). With standardized variables, the unrotated loadings express variable–component correlations. A loading plot therefore answers a different question from a sample-score plot.

Retain the explained-variance table when choosing how many components to interpret. Optional orthogonal Varimax rotation redistributes the retained loading structure using Kaiser normalization (Kaiser, 1958). AquaProv's rotated score coordinates use unit-variance component scores; they are not the original unrotated score values. Rotation can aid description, but it does not identify physical sources without independent evidence.

Ward clustering merges groups to minimize the increase in within-cluster squared dispersion (Ward, 1963). Distance, preprocessing and the requested cluster count jointly define the resulting groups. Report all three, compare cluster summaries with the original chemistry, and examine stability before assigning environmental labels. A dendrogram alone does not establish that a unique natural partition exists.

4.1.3 Bootstrap intervals and observed time series

The ordinary statistical bootstrap resamples retained rows with replacement and summarizes the distribution of the selected statistic (Efron & Tibshirani, 1993). AquaProv's percentile intervals reflect that resampling model. Their interpretation depends on rows being suitable sampling units; a narrow interval from many repeat measurements of a few wells can misrepresent between-well information.

Use the repeated-well assessment when the intended sampling unit is a well. The observed-time-series view connects or groups recorded measurements and does not fit a forecasting model. Trend descriptions must preserve time units, repeated-site identities and missing periods. These restrictions prevent visual continuity from being mistaken for evidence of unobserved temporal behavior.

4.23 Mathematical background of the plots

The following sections explain how the inputs become plotted coordinates, summaries or predictions. Each section names the views that use it. Shared formulas are stated once within this chapter; follow the applicable preprocessing and calculation sections together. Examples labelled illustrative are arithmetic checks, not findings from the field datasets.

4.23.1 The statistical analysis matrix

Applies to: Pearson correlation; Spearman correlation; PCA · 2D sample scores; PCA · loading heatmap; PCA · scree & variance; PCA · correlation circle; HCA · Ward dendrogram; Standardized sample heatmap; Scatter matrix; Standardized boxplots.

Let X contain n retained samples (rows) and p explicitly selected measurements (columns). IDs and coordinate columns should only enter if they are the scientific variables being studied. The default rejects missing measurements and constant columns; explicit drop policies use complete rows across the selected variables and record exclusions. There is no automatic imputation, logarithm, outlier removal or conversion from the ion-unit selector (Jolliffe & Cadima, 2016).

For standardized analyses, AquaProv subtracts each variable's mean and divides by its sample standard deviation:

x̄ⱼ = (1/n)∑ᵢ xᵢⱼ
sⱼ = √[∑ᵢ(xᵢⱼ − x̄ⱼ)²/(n − 1)]
zᵢⱼ = (xᵢⱼ − x̄ⱼ)/sⱼ (4.2)

Center-only PCA/HCA uses xᵢⱼ − x̄ⱼ without division. The standardized sample heatmap and standardized boxplots always use sample z-scores; the scatter matrix displays original units. For the illustrative values 1, 2, 3, the mean is 2, sample SD is 1 and z-scores are −1, 0, 1. Changing the retained rows changes these preprocessing quantities.

4.23.2 Pearson, Spearman and multiple comparisons

Applies to: Pearson correlation; Spearman correlation.

Pearson correlation measures linear association between two retained variables (Pearson, 1895; Spearman, 1904; Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995):

rxy = ∑ᵢ(xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ) / √[∑ᵢ(xᵢ − x̄)² · ∑ᵢ(yᵢ − ȳ)²] (4.3)

Spearman correlation applies Pearson's calculation to within-variable ranks, with average ranks for ties. It measures monotonic association. Both range from −1 to +1; neither proves causation. Every pair uses the same complete-case analysis rows, so the exported n_pairs is constant across the matrix. It is not pairwise deletion.

SciPy supplies two-sided p-values; Spearman uses its asymptotic approximation. When FDR display is selected, AquaProv applies Benjamini–Hochberg once across m = p(p−1)/2 unique off-diagonal tests. With ordered p-values p(k):

q(k) = min[1, minj≥k(m · p(j)/j)] (4.4)

The lower-triangle heatmap displays correlation, with one star below 0.05 and two below 0.01 using either raw p or adjusted q as selected. Diagonal coefficients are 1 and have no test. Small samples, repeated wells and temporal/spatial dependence can invalidate ordinary significance interpretations; changing the star display does not change the coefficients.

4.23.3 PCA by singular value decomposition

Applies to: PCA · 2D sample scores; PCA · loading heatmap; PCA · scree & variance; PCA · correlation circle.

For the centered (optionally standardized) n × p matrix Z, singular value decomposition gives Z = U D Vᵀ. Let dₖ be singular value k and vₖ its variable direction (Jolliffe & Cadima, 2016; Kaiser, 1958):

λₖ = dₖ²/(n − 1)
Scores T = Z Vretained
Loadings L = Vretained diag(√λₖ)
Explained variance (%) = 100λₖ / ∑ⱼλⱼ (4.5)

The retained component count cannot exceed the matrix rank. AquaProv fixes otherwise arbitrary signs by making the largest absolute eigenvector entry positive. Scores locate samples; loadings describe variable relationships with components. Loadings are not the eigenvectors alone. With standardized data, they are correlations between each variable and unrotated PC scores; covariance PCA loadings retain variable-scale effects.

For optional orthogonal Varimax, each loading row is divided by its retained norm (Kaiser normalization), an orthogonal matrix R maximizes the normalized loading simplicity criterion, then row scales are restored:

Lrot = L R
Trot = T diag(1/√λₖ) R
Rotated variance (%) for k = 100∑ⱼLrot,jk² / ∑ⱼλⱼ (4.6)

The rotated score coordinates have unit sample variance; their axis percentages come from rotated loadings. Rotation preserves retained communalities and total retained variance, but its axes are not the original ordered PCs. PCA finds variance directions, not causes or proven source contributions. Review preprocessing, retained variance and sample adequacy with the score plot.

4.23.4 What the sample-score view plots

Applies to: PCA · 2D sample scores.

Each point is a row of T on PC1–PC2, or a row of Trot on RC1–RC2 when rotated. A point at the origin has zero displayed coordinates; it can still differ on omitted components. Distances summarize differences in the retained subspace of the chosen preprocessing, not distances between wells. Colours and symbols identify sample groups and are not additional PCA variables. Read the two axis percentages together to see how much variance the projection displays.

4.23.5 What the loading heatmap plots

Applies to: PCA · loading heatmap.

Cell (j, k) is Lⱼₖ, or Lrot,jk after rotation. A diverging colour scale shows signed magnitude, and cell text is rounded for display. The retained communality for variable j is hⱼ² = ∑ₖLⱼₖ². In standardized PCA this is the fraction of that variable's variance represented by the retained components; in covariance PCA it is in squared variable units. A negative loading is an axis direction, not a negative chemical concentration. Full-precision tables retain values behind the displayed rounding.

4.23.6 Eigenvalues, cumulative variance and retention

Applies to: PCA · scree & variance.

The scree line plots λₖ for every computed component, including those beyond the chosen retained count. The second axis shows 100∑j≤kλⱼ/∑ⱼλⱼ. With p standardized variables the total variance is p (up to numerical error), so eigenvalue = 1 is an optional retention heuristic; it is not a significance threshold. The scree plot always uses the unrotated eigenvalues even when Varimax is requested. Check a proposed elbow against scientific interpretability and stability.

4.23.7 Correlation-circle coordinates

Applies to: PCA · correlation circle.

Each arrow ends at (Lⱼ₁, Lⱼ₂), or the corresponding rotated loadings. This view requires standardized PCA. The unit circle marks x²+y² = 1. The squared arrow length is the portion of variable j's standardized variance represented by these two axes. Short arrows are poorly represented in this plane; their angles can mislead. Similar, opposed and nearly perpendicular arrows suggest positive, negative and weak association only to the extent the displayed plane captures the variables.

4.23.8 Ward clustering on sample vectors

Applies to: HCA · Ward dendrogram.

Ward agglomeration begins with one cluster per sample and repeatedly merges the pair with the smallest increase in within-cluster sum of squares. For clusters A and B with means μA, μB and sizes nA, nB (Ward, 1963):

ΔSS(A,B) = [nAnB/(nA+nB)] ‖μA − μB‖²
SciPy Ward merge height = √(2ΔSS) (4.7)

Distances are Euclidean on the retained observation vectors, standardized if selected. AquaProv does not run Ward on a correlation matrix. The requested cluster count uses an exact merge-count cut; tied heights follow linkage order. Optimal leaf ordering helps readability without changing the merges. Cluster means are exported in the original variable units. Groups depend on variables, scaling and the requested cut and should not automatically be named hydrochemical sources.

4.23.9 Standardized sample heatmap

Applies to: Standardized sample heatmap.

Cell (i, j) is zᵢⱼ = (xᵢⱼ − x̄ⱼ)/sⱼ, with a symmetric diverging scale about zero. A value of +2 is two sample SDs above the retained mean of that variable; −1 is one SD below it. This compares patterns across variables with different units. It is not a concentration map, correlation matrix or health classification. Samples retain their analysis order; no clustering/reordering is implied by adjacent rows. If labels are thinned for readability, all sample rows remain plotted and exported.

4.23.10 Pairwise scatter panels and marginal histograms

Applies to: Scatter matrix.

Off-diagonal panel (j, k) plots retained pairs (xᵢₖ, xᵢⱼ) in the original measurement units. Diagonal panels show each variable's histogram. Shared complete-case filtering is performed before any panel, so the same samples appear across the matrix. Although a standardized table is also exported, it is not the plotted coordinate table for this view. The matrix adds no regression line, correlation test, log transform or outlier removal. Curvature, clusters and extreme observations can explain why one summary correlation is inadequate.

4.23.11 Quartiles, whiskers and individual extremes

Applies to: Standardized boxplots.

For a set of finite plotted values v, let Q₁, Q₂ and Q₃ be the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles. AquaProv's NumPy/Matplotlib path uses linear quantiles. If sorted values are v₍₀₎…v₍ₙ₋₁₎, a quantile q lies at index (n−1)q and linearly interpolates neighbouring values.

IQR = Q₃ − Q₁
Lower fence = Q₁ − 1.5 IQR;   upper fence = Q₃ + 1.5 IQR (4.8)

The box spans Q₁ to Q₃ with a median line. Whiskers reach the most extreme observations within the fences, not necessarily the fence coordinates. Values beyond the whiskers remain observations; the plot does not remove them. For values 1, 2, 3, 4, 9, Q₁ = 2, median = 3, Q₃ = 4, and the upper fence is 7, so 9 is beyond the whisker. This descriptive range is not a confidence interval.

4.23.12 Which values enter the standardized boxplots

Applies to: Standardized boxplots.

Each variable's box uses its sample z-scores, not its raw concentrations. Zero is the variable mean; the median need not be zero for an asymmetric sample. Standardization gives each variable sample SD 1 but does not make its distribution normal. Different-looking boxes therefore reveal shape and relative extremes after differences in units and spread are removed.

4.23.13 Observed time series: ordering rather than modelling

Applies to: Time series · observed measurements.

Each plotted point is a supplied pair (tᵢ, vᵢ). AquaProv parses the explicitly selected date format, retains complete date/measurement pairs according to the chosen policy, and sorts them stably by site/group and time. It draws straight segments between successive observations:

Displayed segment: v(t) = vᵢ + (vᵢ₊₁ − vᵢ)(t − tᵢ)/(tᵢ₊₁ − tᵢ), for distinct adjacent times (4.9)

This equation describes the connecting line only; the software does not produce estimated measurements between dates. Duplicate dates are shown individually without aggregation. Choose a site column so different wells are not connected as one trajectory. Gaps in sampling, changing well coverage and seasonal timing affect interpretation; no trend significance, smoothing, forecast or causal model is inferred.

4.23.14 Measurements against source-row positions

Applies to: Measured series - source rows.

For source row r = 1,…,n and series j = 0,…,m−1, the vertical coordinate is the supplied measurement vᵣⱼ. A chosen horizontal separation δ distinguishes overlapping series:

xᵣⱼ = r + [j − (m−1)/2]δ;   yᵣⱼ = vᵣⱼ (4.10)

For two series and δ = 0.36, row 10 appears at X = 9.82 and 10.18. Both positions still refer to source row 10. The offset changes display positions only. No dates, fitted trend or interpolated measurements are created. Compare quantities with compatible units, or label their differing units clearly; the plot does not convert them.

Missing values are omitted separately in each series while preserving the original row positions. Reported zeros remain exactly zero and can use open markers. Non-numeric and infinite measurements must be resolved before plotting. An optional horizontal threshold is entered in measurement units and does not classify a water sample or establish compliance by itself.

4.23.15 Independent-row percentile bootstrap

Applies to: Uncertainty · bootstrap interval.

For n retained measurements, AquaProv draws B replacement samples of size n. It computes the selected mean or median θ*b from each sample. The observed estimate θ̂ is calculated on the original retained data (Efron & Tibshirani, 1993):

Confidence interval = [Qα/2(θ*), Q1−α/2(θ*)]
α = 1 − confidence/100
Bootstrap SE = sample SD of θ*₁,…,θ*B (4.11)

The histogram contains the B statistic estimates, not the original measurements. At 95% confidence the interval uses the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles (NumPy linear quantiles). Defaults are 2,000 resamples and seed 42; the accepted range is 200–20,000 with at least three complete observations. The seed makes a given configuration reproducible, not scientifically certain.

The interval concerns sampling variation of a mean or median under independent, representative rows. It is neither a prediction interval for a new measurement nor propagation of laboratory SD. Repeated observations at wells require an appropriate grouped design such as the well-bootstrap assessment. Constant observations produce a degenerate interval; small samples may give poor coverage.

5.1 Interpolation assumptions and quantitative interpretation

Spatial interpolation estimates a value at an unsampled location from observations and an explicit spatial rule. Inverse-distance weighting emphasizes nearby samples through a normalized power of distance (Shepard, 1968). For observed values vi, positive distances di and power p:

wi = di−p/Σjdj−p;   v̂(s0) = Σiwivi (5.1)

At an exact sample coordinate, AquaProv returns the observed value. Otherwise its nonnegative weights sum to one, so the estimate lies within the observed value range. For two illustrative values 10 and 30 at distances 1 and 2, power 2 gives an estimate of 14. IDW provides no model-based prediction variance; a smooth field is not evidence of low uncertainty.

Ordinary kriging instead uses a covariance model and a sum-to-one constraint to estimate an unknown locally constant mean (Cressie, 1993). AquaProv's Maps module accepts user-supplied range, structured partial sill and nugget. The partial sill excludes the nugget. In the exponential model the entered range is a practical range, at which the structured covariance is about 5% of its value at zero separation.

Kw + 1μ = c;   1Tw = 1
v̂(s0) = wTv;   σ2pred = s + n0 − wTc − μ (5.2)

Here K is the observation covariance matrix including diagonal nugget n0, c is the structured covariance with the target, s is partial sill and μ is the Lagrange multiplier in this sign convention. AquaProv reports variance for a new observation, including the nugget. Kriging weights may be negative, and with a nonzero nugget the prediction at a sampled coordinate need not equal that measurement. These details affect interpretation of both contours and variance maps.

Distances must be calculated in an appropriate metric coordinate system. Geographic degrees are angular coordinates and cannot be treated as uniform metres. 5.15.2 Coordinates used for distances

Applies to: Kriging contours; Kriging prediction variance; IDW contours.

Geographic location and distance are separate operations. Projected input uses its source distance units. For WGS84 longitude / latitude → local metres, AquaProv converts degrees to a local metric approximation about the mean longitude λ₀ and latitude φ₀:

x = R(λ − λ₀)cos φ₀;   y = R(φ − φ₀)
R = 6,371,008.8 m; angles in radians
dᵢⱼ = √[(xᵢ − xⱼ)² + (yᵢ − yⱼ)²] (5.3)

The local approximation is restricted to extents no greater than 3° in either direction and latitudes within ±85°. For larger or more precise studies, choose a suitable projected CRS and consistent distance units. Changing the basemap for display does not change the analysis distance coordinates. Duplicate locations and fewer than three non-collinear observations cannot define these interpolated surfaces without deliberate data preparation.

The current Distance coordinates control also offers automatic UTM/UPS and a custom projected CRS in metres. 5.15.3 Ordinary kriging: model, weights and variance

Applies to: Kriging contours; Kriging prediction variance.

Ordinary kriging assumes an unknown locally constant mean and an isotropic covariance depending only on distance. Let a be range, s the structured partial sill, and n₀ the nugget, all in consistent distance or squared measurement units. With r = d/a (Cressie, 1993):

Spherical C(d) = s(1 − 1.5r + 0.5r³) for r < 1; otherwise 0
Exponential C(d) = s exp(−3r) (5.4)

For the exponential model, a is the practical range where structured covariance is s exp(−3), about 5% of s. Form Kᵢⱼ = C(dᵢⱼ) + n₀δᵢⱼ, cᵢ = C(‖sᵢ−s₀‖), and the ones vector 1. The unscaled equivalent of AquaProv's linear system is:

K w + 1μ = c;   1ᵀw = 1
v̂(s₀) = wᵀv
σ²pred(s₀) = s + n₀ − wᵀc − μ (5.5)

μ is the Lagrange multiplier under this covariance sign convention. The implementation scales covariances by s+n₀ to improve numerical conditioning. The sum-to-one constraint preserves a constant mean. Weights may be negative, so predictions need not stay within observed values. The reported variance is for a new observation, including the nugget; with a nonzero nugget, a coincident target is not forced to reproduce an observed sample exactly.

The Maps module uses user-supplied range, partial sill and nugget and does not fit a variogram automatically. Fitted models are provided separately in Assessment. Neither module silently estimates anisotropy or removes a trend. Validate the model with held-out data before using its interpolated pattern scientifically.

5.15.4 Reading a prediction-variance map

Applies to: Kriging prediction variance.

Grid colour is σ²pred from the kriging system, in squared measurement units. Its square root is conditional prediction SD in measurement units. For fixed covariance parameters, the variance depends on locations and the covariance model, not directly on measured concentration magnitude. It can be high where spatial support is weak, but it does not measure pollution (Cressie, 1993).

This uncertainty is conditional on the selected variogram and stationarity assumptions; parameter uncertainty, missing processes and errors in coordinates are not included. The convex-hull mask limits the exported analysis domain. The standard Maps variance view does not independently establish a calibrated confidence interval or exceedance probability.

5.15.5 Inverse-distance weighting (IDW)

Applies to: IDW contours.

At target s₀, IDW calculates a normalized weighted average of the selected neighbouring sample values vᵢ. Nearest neighbours → All samples includes all eligible observations; a positive count selects the nearest available observations and includes ties at the cutoff distance. With distance dᵢ = ‖s₀ − sᵢ‖ and positive power p, the sums run over that selected set (Shepard, 1968):

wᵢ = dᵢ−p / ∑ⱼdⱼ−p
v̂(s₀) = ∑ᵢwᵢvᵢ (5.6)

If the target exactly matches a sample, its observed value is returned. Internally distances are normalized by the nearest distance before exponentiation for numerical stability; normalized weights are unchanged. All weights are nonnegative and sum to 1, so predictions stay within the sample value range. Increasing p favours nearby samples more strongly. At distances 1 and 2 with p = 2, illustrative values 10 and 30 yield (10 + 30/4)/(1 + 1/4) = 14.

The grid is restricted to the convex hull, and to the study-boundary intersection when selected, unless boundary extrapolation is explicitly enabled. Grid resolution changes evaluation density, not the number of observations. IDW supplies no prediction variance or probability. Boundaries clip the display; they are not physical groundwater-flow barriers.

6.11.2 Piecewise-linear XYZ interpolation

Applies to: XYZ interpolated surface.

The surface triangulates the observed X–Y coordinates with Delaunay triangles. Within one triangle, a target is a barycentric combination of its three vertices:

(x,y) = α₁(x₁,y₁) + α₂(x₂,y₂) + α₃(x₃,y₃)
α₁ + α₂ + α₃ = 1;   αᵢ ≥ 0
ẑ(x,y) = α₁z₁ + α₂z₂ + α₃z₃ (6.1)

Thus each triangle is a plane passing through the three observations. For equal barycentric weights and vertex Z values 10, 20, 30, the target value is 20. Refinement produces smaller render triangles on the same piecewise-linear surface; it does not add observations or fit a smoother model. Outside the sample convex hull values remain undefined. Duplicate or collinear X–Y locations must be resolved. X and Y remain in source units; no geographic distance correction is inferred. The surface provides no prediction variance.

6.11.3 The statistical analysis matrix

Applies to: PCA · 3D sample scores; PCA · 3D component loadings.

Let X contain n retained samples (rows) and p explicitly selected measurements (columns). IDs and coordinate columns should only enter if they are the scientific variables being studied. The default rejects missing measurements and constant columns; explicit drop policies use complete rows across the selected variables and record exclusions. There is no automatic imputation, logarithm, outlier removal or conversion from the ion-unit selector (Jolliffe & Cadima, 2016).

For standardized analyses, AquaProv subtracts each variable's mean and divides by its sample standard deviation:

x̄ⱼ = (1/n)∑ᵢ xᵢⱼ
sⱼ = √[∑ᵢ(xᵢⱼ − x̄ⱼ)²/(n − 1)]
zᵢⱼ = (xᵢⱼ − x̄ⱼ)/sⱼ (6.2)

Center-only PCA/HCA uses xᵢⱼ − x̄ⱼ without division. The standardized sample heatmap and standardized boxplots always use sample z-scores; the scatter matrix displays original units. For the illustrative values 1, 2, 3, the mean is 2, sample SD is 1 and z-scores are −1, 0, 1. Changing the retained rows changes these preprocessing quantities.

6.11.4 PCA by singular value decomposition

Applies to: PCA · 3D sample scores; PCA · 3D component loadings.

For the centered (optionally standardized) n × p matrix Z, singular value decomposition gives Z = U D Vᵀ. Let dₖ be singular value k and vₖ its variable direction (Jolliffe & Cadima, 2016; Kaiser, 1958):

λₖ = dₖ²/(n − 1)
Scores T = Z Vretained
Loadings L = Vretained diag(√λₖ)
Explained variance (%) = 100λₖ / ∑ⱼλⱼ (6.3)

The retained component count cannot exceed the matrix rank. AquaProv fixes otherwise arbitrary signs by making the largest absolute eigenvector entry positive. Scores locate samples; loadings describe variable relationships with components. Loadings are not the eigenvectors alone. With standardized data, they are correlations between each variable and unrotated PC scores; covariance PCA loadings retain variable-scale effects.

For optional orthogonal Varimax, each loading row is divided by its retained norm (Kaiser normalization), an orthogonal matrix R maximizes the normalized loading simplicity criterion, then row scales are restored:

Lrot = L R
Trot = T diag(1/√λₖ) R
Rotated variance (%) for k = 100∑ⱼLrot,jk² / ∑ⱼλⱼ (6.4)

The rotated score coordinates have unit sample variance; their axis percentages come from rotated loadings. Rotation preserves retained communalities and total retained variance, but its axes are not the original ordered PCs. PCA finds variance directions, not causes or proven source contributions. Review preprocessing, retained variance and sample adequacy with the score plot.

7.1 Uncertainty is conditional on a measurement model

A probabilistic output requires assumptions about which measurements vary, their distributions and their dependence. Sensitivity analysis examines how conclusions change when those assumptions change (Pianosi et al., 2016). AquaProv distinguishes fixed measurements, a chosen relative-SD scenario and laboratory-SD propagation. A selected relative error is an analyst's scenario and must not be described as laboratory-calibrated uncertainty.

For a positive detected concentration with arithmetic mean m and SD s, AquaProv uses a moment-matched lognormal distribution. Its parameters on the natural-log scale are:

σ2log = ln[1 + (s/m)2];   μlog = ln(m) − σ2log/2
C* = exp(μlog + σlogZ),   Z ∼ Normal(0, 1) (7.1)

This convention preserves the specified arithmetic mean and variance; it does not place the measured mean at the lognormal median. Laboratory mode requires usable mapped SDs. A zero mean with positive SD cannot be matched by a nonnegative distribution and remains unassessed under that model. The optional shared log-error correlation is an explicit dependence scenario, not a correlation estimated from the concentration table.

Censored results carry a reporting limit and a status rather than a known concentration. Exclusion, a uniform draw between zero and the limit, and fixed half-limit substitution answer different conditional questions. AquaProv compares these scenarios separately and does not assign them posterior probabilities. Report how many measurements were censored and whether a conclusion changes across treatments before selecting a preferred interpretation (Pianosi et al., 2016).

7.1.1 Probability, simulation precision and chemical screening

For B usable draws, an event probability is the number of draws satisfying the event divided by B. AquaProv also exports simulation-precision diagnostics, including Wilson bounds and split-half differences. The familiar binomial Monte Carlo standard error illustrates why more draws reduce numerical variability:

P̂(event) = Σb=1…B1(event in draw b)/B;   SEMC = √[P̂(1−P̂)/B] (7.2)

For an illustrative probability of 0.5 from 2,000 draws, this standard error is approximately 0.0112. Increasing the draw count improves simulation precision but does not validate an assumed SD, censoring model or distribution. Likewise, facies probabilities describe uncertainty in a chemical classification, not probabilities that competing geological processes occurred.

The bundled selected-chemical screen uses explicitly named values from World Health Organization (2022). Its four-analyte profile and reporting bases are listed later in this chapter. A threshold probability is conditional on the input model and selected objective; omitted hazards and unassessed chemicals remain outside that probability. The result must not be described as a complete drinking-water compliance determination.

7.1.2 Compositional analysis and cluster stability

Concentration components can be compared through log ratios when relative composition is the scientific target. Isometric log-ratio coordinates provide an orthonormal representation of that information (Egozcue et al., 2003), with a hydrochemical application discussed by Owen et al. (2016). AquaProv projects centered log ratios into a Helmert basis:

g(x) = exp[(1/D)Σjln xj];   clrj(x) = ln[xj/g(x)]
ilr(x) = clr(x)B;   BTB = I;   BT1 = 0 (7.3)

The D−1 ILR coordinates are centered without variance standardization before PCA. Multiplying every component of a sample by the same positive constant leaves its coordinates unchanged. Selected parts must be positive and nonoverlapping; pH, EC, TDS and alkalinity are not treated as parts in this analysis. AquaProv excludes unsuitable rows with an audit and does not add a pseudocount. Its exported CLR loading directions also differ from the variance-scaled loadings of ordinary PCA.

Bootstrap co-assignment examines how often eligible sample pairs remain together under resampling, extending the resampling principle of Efron and Tibshirani (1993). High co-assignment supports stability under the chosen procedure, rather than proof of a unique aquifer facies. Report the retained parts, row exclusions, cluster count and resampling configuration beside the stability result.

7.1.3 Spatial validation and repeated wells

Assessment's spatial-validation workflow holds out geographic blocks and fits variogram parameters using training observations only. The full-data diagnostic fit is distinct from those holdout fits. Compare held-out RMSE, MAE and bias against the IDW and training-mean baselines, and report interval coverage where available. This tests a prediction procedure under the selected spatial partition; the uncertainty remains conditional on its covariance and sampling assumptions (Cressie, 1993).

For repeated wells, AquaProv first computes each well's available mean and then gives each well equal weight. This target differs from pooling all rows, which gives frequently sampled wells more influence. It resamples whole-well means for percentile intervals (Efron & Tibshirani, 1993). Descriptive within-well slopes use the median of pairwise slopes when sufficient distinct times are present, following Sen (1968). These intervals and slopes do not remove spatial dependence or supply a groundwater forecast.

7.22.1 From laboratory concentrations to chemical equivalents

Applies to: Measurement quality & charge balance; SAR & classification uncertainty; Piper composition intervals.

A mass concentration gives the mass of an ion; an equivalent concentration also accounts for its electric charge. Let Cᵢ be concentration in mg/L, Mᵢ molar mass in g/mol (numerically also mg/mmol), and |zᵢ| the absolute ionic charge (Piper, 1944):

mᵢ = Cᵢ / Mᵢ (mmol/L)
eᵢ = |zᵢ| Cᵢ / Mᵢ (meq/L) (7.4)

For an input already in meq/L, eᵢ is the supplied value. To recover mmol/L from meq/L, divide by |zᵢ|. These conversions are different for divalent ions such as Ca²⁺ and monovalent ions such as Na⁺.

7.22.2 Charge balance and measurement-status counts

Applies to: Measurement quality & charge balance.

Charge-balance error compares measured charge totals, without forcing them to agree:

CBE (%) = 100(Tc − Ta)/(Tc + Ta) (7.5)

Both totals use meq/L and all eight core ions must be measured. Positive CBE means excess measured cation charge; negative means excess measured anion charge. The default review flag is |CBE| > 5%; the user can set the tolerance. For Tc = 5 and Ta = 4 meq/L, CBE = 11.111%. A small CBE can result from offsetting analytical errors and is not proof of accuracy.

The assessment also exports a separate CBE including nitrate and fluoride when both are measured; it does not silently add these to the core-ion diagnostic. Missing CO₃ is not assumed zero. The status bar chart counts analyte observations (cells) by detected, zero, missing, censored or other parsed status, rather than counting only sample rows. Inspect species/reporting-basis conversion and exclusions before interpreting downstream plots.

7.22.3 SAR and conductivity-dependent USSL boundaries

Applies to: SAR & classification uncertainty.

Using Na, Ca and Mg in meq/L, AquaProv calculates sodium adsorption ratio (SAR) (US Salinity Laboratory Staff, 1954):

SAR = eNa / √[(eCa + eMg)/2] (7.6)

Ca+Mg must be positive. The horizontal axis is measured EC referenced to 25 °C, expressed in µS/cm; 1 mS/cm = 1 dS/m = 1,000 µS/cm. No TDS-to-EC estimate or temperature correction is performed. The salinity class changes at EC = 250, 750 and 2,250 µS/cm. Sodium-hazard boundaries are curves, with E denoting the numerical EC value in µS/cm:

S1/S2: SAR = 18.87 − 4.44 log₁₀E
S2/S3: SAR = 31.31 − 6.66 log₁₀E
S3/S4: SAR = 43.75 − 8.87 log₁₀E (7.7)

Each crossed boundary increases the sodium class; exact ties use the higher class. The combined reference domain is 100 ≤ EC ≤ 5,000 and 0 ≤ SAR ≤ 32. Values outside it are reported explicitly. Review flags mark points within 0.5 SAR of a sodium curve or 25 µS/cm of a salinity cut; these are review tolerances, not uncertainty intervals.

Illustrative check: Ca = 2, Mg = 2 and Na = 2 meq/L give SAR = 1.414. At EC = 1,000, the S1/S2 boundary is 5.55 and the point is C3–S1. Field suitability also depends on soil, crop, drainage and irrigation management.

7.22.5 Conditional Monte Carlo measurement propagation

Applies to: SAR & classification uncertainty; Piper composition intervals; Chemical threshold probabilities; Follow-up assay priorities; Sensitivity across analysis choices.

For a positive detected concentration with supplied mean m and SD s, AquaProv uses a moment-matched lognormal marginal. Laboratory mode uses mapped SDs, scenario mode uses s = r·m for the selected relative SD r, and fixed mode uses a constant (Pianosi et al., 2016):

σ²log = ln[1 + (s/m)²]
μlog = ln(m) − σ²log/2
C* = exp(μlog + σlogZ),   Z ∼ Normal(0,1) (7.9)

An optional shared log-error correlation ρ uses Zᵢ = √ρ Z₀ + √(1−ρ)εᵢ, with independent standard normal Z₀ and εᵢ. This is a specified log-error scenario, not an estimated concentration correlation. Zero with positive SD has no supported nonnegative moment-matched distribution; a missing laboratory SD is unassessed in laboratory mode.

Each draw is converted to equivalents and passed through the same SAR, sodium-percentage and graphical classification formulas as the deterministic diagrams. Reported intervals are the finite-draw 2.5th, 50th and 97.5th percentiles. Class and threshold probabilities are indicator frequencies:

P̂(event) = (1/B)∑b=1…B 1(event in draw b)
Monte Carlo SE = √[P̂(1−P̂)/B] (7.10)

The exported 95% Wilson bounds and split-half difference describe simulation precision. They do not quantify whether the chosen SDs, distributions or correlation are scientifically correct. The irrigation interval view plots SAR and Na% intervals by sample; class probabilities are in the result tables. All conclusions are conditional on the chosen measurement and censoring model.

7.22.6 Normalize cations and anions separately

Applies to: Piper composition intervals.

Using equivalents eᵢ, form the two charge totals and the six grouped fractions (Piper, 1944):

Tc = eCa + eMg + eNa + eK
Ta = eHCO₃ + eCO₃ + eCl + eSO₄
c = (eCa, eMg, eNa + eK) / Tc
a = (eHCO₃ + eCO₃, eSO₄, eCl) / Ta (7.11)

Below, c₁, c₂, c₃ mean Ca, Mg and Na+K fractions, and a₁, a₂, a₃ mean HCO₃+CO₃, SO₄ and Cl fractions. Each triplet sums to 1; multiply by 100 for equivalent percentages. Both totals must be positive. AquaProv checks this before constructing normalized major-ion diagrams.

Normalization explains why these diagrams describe relative composition. Multiplying every concentration in a sample by ten leaves its position unchanged. Separate normalization also hides disagreement between cation and anion totals, so a plausible plotted position does not establish good charge balance.

7.22.7 Piper intervals and dominant-ion facies probabilities

Applies to: Piper composition intervals.

Each Monte Carlo draw is separately normalized to cation and anion fractions. Assessment uses unit-triangle coordinates (c₃+c₂/2, hc₂) and (a₃+a₂/2, ha₂), with h = √3/2. Its unshifted diamond coordinates are (Piper, 1944; Pianosi et al., 2016):

xd = (c₃ + a₂ + a₃)/2
yd = h(1 − c₃ + a₂ + a₃) (7.12)

These normalized coordinates differ in page placement from the conventional three-panel Piper layout. A facies is named for a cation group and an anion group only when each respective maximum is greater than 50%; otherwise that half is called mixed. At exactly 50%, the strict rule is mixed. Facies probability is its frequency across draws, with invalid compositions retained as undefined.

The figure shows marginal x and y 95% intervals around coordinate medians for up to 150 assessable rows; full tables include all rows. Horizontal and vertical error bars are not a joint 95% region, a fitted ellipse or a reaction path. All eight measured major ions are needed; absence of carbonate data is not evidence for zero carbonate.

7.22.8 Conditional probability of an upper-threshold exceedance

Applies to: Chemical threshold probabilities; Follow-up assay priorities.

For selected species concentration C and a named threshold H in mg/L of that same species, the event is strictly C > H. Its probability is the fraction of simulation draws above H. A draw exactly equal to H is not an exceedance. For example, 120 exceedances among 2,000 draws give P̂ = 0.06 and Monte Carlo SE ≈ 0.00531 (World Health Organization, 2022).

The screening figure shows up to 30 assessable assay probabilities in its left panel; its right histogram uses all assessable probabilities with bins from 0 to 1. No supported distribution means unassessed, not P = 0. The default versioned assessment profile is WHO-2022-selected-chemicals-v1 (NO₃ 50, F 1.5, total Cr 0.05 and Ni 0.07 mg/L); it is distinct from the configurable WQI standards profiles. Review the actual exported profile and reporting basis.

For censored observations, logical bounds can sometimes be reported without a distribution: C < L with L ≤ H implies no exceedance under that reporting inequality; C > L with L > H implies exceedance. Otherwise the probability may remain anywhere in [0,1]. Those bounds assume a valid reporting limit. Screening a few chemical species does not establish overall drinking-water safety.

7.22.9 Follow-up assay priorities and the budget rule

Applies to: Follow-up assay priorities.

The default ambiguity priority is largest near P = 0.5 and zero at P = 0 or 1:

Ambiguity = min(P, 1 − P)
Priority score = ambiguity / assay cost
Budget eligibility: rank × equal assay cost ≤ budget, with priority score > 0 (7.13)

Candidates are sorted by descending score, then ascending relative threshold margin |median−H|/H, source row and analyte. The left panel displays exceedance probabilities in this priority order; the right displays scores, with budget-eligible assays highlighted. High exceedance probability and high uncertainty are different reasons to act. This default score is a review heuristic, not a health-risk or value-of-information calculation.

The optional EVSI method uses a normal current log concentration X ∼ N(μ,σ²) and an independent follow-up log assay Y|X ∼ N(X,τ²). Its posterior has gain k = σ²/(σ²+τ²), mean μ′ = μ+k(Y−μ), and variance σ′² = (1−k)σ². With unit loss for an incorrect binary threshold decision:

Current risk = min(P, 1−P)
EVSI = current risk − EY[min(P′, 1−P′)]
EVSI priority = EVSI / assay cost (7.14)

The expectation uses numerical Gaussian integration in expected_value. Equal assay costs, stable chemistry, additive binary loss and no information sharing across assays are assumed. Unsupported censored current distributions cannot receive this EVSI rank. Inspect excluded assays as well as the ranked list.

7.22.10 Sensitivity to uncertainty and censoring assumptions

Applies to: Sensitivity across analysis choices.

The comparison runs four alternatives: fixed detected values with censored cells excluded; relative-SD draws with censored cells excluded; relative-SD draws with C ∼ Uniform(0,L) for left-censored cells; and relative-SD draws with fixed C = L/2. Uniform censoring has mean L/2 and variance L²/12, whereas half-limit substitution has no within-limit variability (Pianosi et al., 2016).

Scenario probability range = [minsP̂s(C>H), maxsP̂s(C>H)] (7.15)

The range plot shows up to 40 assessable assays and the bar chart summarizes conclusion labels across scenarios. “Stable below” requires all four scenarios to be assessable with every P ≤ 0.05; “stable above” requires every P ≥ 0.95. Other fully assessed cases are scenario-dependent or ambiguous, and incomplete cases have insufficient information. These are display rules, not regulatory thresholds. The span is not a probability-weighted average or a confidence interval: the software assigns no probabilities to the assumptions themselves. For a separate propagation run, a supplied lognormal model truncated below L is also supported, but it is not one of these four comparison cases.

7.22.11 Compositional log-ratio PCA

Applies to: Compositional PCA; Compositional cluster stability.

For D positive, non-overlapping concentration components xᵢ, the geometric mean g(x) removes the arbitrary overall concentration scale. AquaProv computes centered log-ratios and projects them into an orthonormal Helmert basis B of the zero-sum subspace (Egozcue et al., 2003):

g(x) = exp[(1/D)∑ⱼln xⱼ]
clrⱼ(x) = ln[xⱼ/g(x)]
ilr(x) = clr(x) B;   BᵀB = I,   Bᵀ1 = 0
dA(x,y) = ‖ilr(x) − ilr(y)‖₂ (7.16)

The D−1 ILR coordinates are centered, not standardized, before SVD PCA. Sample scores use U DSVD, and eigenvalues are dₖ²/(n−1). The exported clr_loadings are B V, directions in log-ratio space; they are not the variance-scaled loadings from the ordinary PCA module. Ward clustering uses Euclidean distance on ILR coordinates (Aitchison distance). The left plot shows PC1–PC2 scores coloured by Ward cluster, and the compositional variance panel shows component percentages.

At least three parts and four strictly positive complete samples are needed. Zero, missing and censored components are excluded with an audit; no pseudocount is added. pH, EC, TDS and alkalinity are not compositional parts here, and total Cr must not overlap with Cr(VI). Multiplying a sample by any positive constant leaves its log ratios unchanged; selected components determine the subcomposition and interpretation.

7.22.12 Bootstrap cluster co-assignment

Applies to: Compositional cluster stability.

For each of B row-bootstrap resamples, AquaProv fits Ward clusters to the resampled ILR observations, calculates their centroids, and assigns all original rows to the nearest resampled centroid. The consensus matrix is (Efron & Tibshirani, 1993; Ward, 1963):

Aᵢⱼ = (1/B)∑b 1[assigned_clusterb(i) = assigned_clusterb(j)] (7.17)

It is symmetric, lies in [0,1], and has diagonal 1. A value of 0.9 means the pair was assigned together in 90% of bootstrap runs. The heatmap orders rows by their original cluster for readability; the exported matrix preserves identifiers. Per-resample adjusted Rand indices compare the whole resampled partition with the original, correcting agreement for chance.

These frequencies depend on the fixed variable selection, cluster count and nearest-centroid assignment rule. They are not probabilities that a hydrogeological grouping is true. Row resampling assumes independent samples; the implementation rejects repeated well identifiers for this stability route. Use one scientifically justified campaign per well or an appropriate grouped inference design.

7.22.13 Coordinates used for distances

Applies to: Spatial validation & variogram; Fitted kriging & conditional uncertainty.

Geographic location and distance are separate operations. Projected input uses its source distance units. For WGS84 longitude / latitude → local metres, AquaProv converts degrees to a local metric approximation about the mean longitude λ₀ and latitude φ₀:

x = R(λ − λ₀)cos φ₀;   y = R(φ − φ₀)
R = 6,371,008.8 m; angles in radians
dᵢⱼ = √[(xᵢ − xⱼ)² + (yᵢ − yⱼ)²] (7.18)

The local approximation is restricted to extents no greater than 3° in either direction and latitudes within ±85°. For larger or more precise studies, choose a suitable projected CRS and consistent distance units. Changing the basemap for display does not change the analysis distance coordinates. Duplicate locations and fewer than three non-collinear observations cannot define these interpolated surfaces without deliberate data preparation.

The current Distance coordinates control also offers automatic UTM/UPS and a custom projected CRS in metres. These transform WGS84 positions through the selected projection before calculating distances; they do not use the local equation above. Enter Analysis CRS for the custom mode, and retain the selected CRS in the calculation record. For existing projected X/Y, Source CRS override declares the original projected system.

Choose Grid definition → Cells per axis for 20–1,000 cells per axis, or Cell size in analysis units for explicit spacing in the analysis coordinate units. The cell-size option has a 1,000,000-point limit. Resolution changes where predictions are evaluated; it does not increase sample support. Colour classification and Surface drawing change the presentation. Retain exported class boundaries when comparing Equal intervals, Quantiles, Natural Breaks (Jenks) or custom classes; large-grid Natural Breaks uses a bounded histogram approximation.

7.22.14 Ordinary kriging: model, weights and variance

Applies to: Spatial validation & variogram; Fitted kriging & conditional uncertainty.

Ordinary kriging assumes an unknown locally constant mean and an isotropic covariance depending only on distance. Let a be range, s the structured partial sill, and n₀ the nugget, all in consistent distance or squared measurement units. With r = d/a (Cressie, 1993):

Spherical C(d) = s(1 − 1.5r + 0.5r³) for r < 1; otherwise 0
Exponential C(d) = s exp(−3r) (7.19)

For the exponential model, a is the practical range where structured covariance is s exp(−3), about 5% of s. Form Kᵢⱼ = C(dᵢⱼ) + n₀δᵢⱼ, cᵢ = C(‖sᵢ−s₀‖), and the ones vector 1. The unscaled equivalent of AquaProv's linear system is:

K w + 1μ = c;   1ᵀw = 1
v̂(s₀) = wᵀv
σ²pred(s₀) = s + n₀ − wᵀc − μ (7.20)

μ is the Lagrange multiplier under this covariance sign convention. The implementation scales covariances by s+n₀ to improve numerical conditioning. The sum-to-one constraint preserves a constant mean. Weights may be negative, so predictions need not stay within observed values. The reported variance is for a new observation, including the nugget; with a nonzero nugget, a coincident target is not forced to reproduce an observed sample exactly.

The Maps module uses user-supplied range, partial sill and nugget and does not fit a variogram automatically. Fitted models are provided separately in Assessment. Neither module silently estimates anisotropy or removes a trend. Validate the model with held-out data before using its interpolated pattern scientifically.

7.22.15 Training-only variograms and spatial block validation

Applies to: Spatial validation & variogram; Fitted kriging & conditional uncertainty.

Assessment creates 3–10 geographic K-means blocks using coordinates only. Each block is held out in turn, and its variogram is fitted using the remaining observations. The empirical Matheron semivariance in a distance bin is (Cressie, 1993):

γ̂(h) = [1/(2Nh)]∑(i,j) in bin h(vᵢ − vⱼ)² (7.21)

Ten equal-width bins extend to 0.8 times the maximum training separation, with at least three pairs per retained bin and at least four populated bins for fitting. Bounded least squares fits γ(h) = s+n₀−C(h), weighting each residual by √(Nh/∑Nh) and scaling it by the training sample variance. Range, sill and nugget are refitted for every fold. The full-data variogram shown beside held-out predictions is a separate diagnostic; it is never used for those holdouts.

For held-out errors eᵢ = predictionᵢ − observationᵢ:

RMSE = √[(1/n)∑ᵢeᵢ²];   MAE = (1/n)∑ᵢ|eᵢ|;   bias = (1/n)∑ᵢeᵢ
Conditional 95% interval = prediction ± 1.96√variance (7.22)

The exported residual column uses the opposite sign (observed − prediction); exported bias follows the equation above. IDW (power 2) and the training mean provide baselines. Coverage is the fraction of holdouts in their intervals. With a selected threshold, Brier score is mean squared difference between predicted exceedance probability and the observed exceedance indicator.

The plot compares observed and held-out predicted values with a 1:1 reference line. It requires at least max(15, 3 × blocks) observations; individual training fits need at least eight varying observations. Blocks have no separation buffer. Fits reaching parameter bounds, cross-boundary dependence and Gaussian intervals below zero need scientific review.

7.22.16 Fitted prediction map and conditional uncertainty

Applies to: Fitted kriging & conditional uncertainty.

After spatial block validation, this route fits the variogram to the full retained data and applies ordinary kriging to a hull-masked grid. The left panel displays predictions. The right displays conditional SD = √variance unless an upper threshold H is configured, in which case it displays (Cressie, 1993):

P(v > H | fitted model) = 1 − Φ[(H − v̂)/σpred] (7.23)

Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution. If SD is zero, the result is the indicator v̂ > H. These model-based probabilities differ from the measurement Monte Carlo probabilities elsewhere in Assessment. They assume Gaussian prediction errors and omit fitted-parameter uncertainty. Read holdout RMSE, coverage and parameter-bound flags before interpreting the map; smoother or denser grids are not additional validation.

7.22.17 Bootstrap wells as sampling units

Applies to: Well bootstrap & descriptive trends.

Within each well j, calculate the mean mⱼ of its available measured values. The target is the equally weighted mean over J wells (Efron & Tibshirani, 1993; Sen, 1968):

θ̂well = (1/J)∑ⱼmⱼ
θ̂pooled = ∑ⱼnⱼmⱼ / ∑ⱼnⱼ (7.24)

The two estimates differ when wells have unequal sampling counts. The bootstrap resamples J whole-well means with replacement and averages each draw; its 95% interval is the 2.5th–97.5th percentile range. At least three distinct wells are required. This addresses between-well sampling variation, conditional on the observed well summaries; it does not estimate laboratory error or remove spatial dependence among wells.

If time is mapped, repeated measurements at each identical time are first averaged within a well. With at least three distinct times, the descriptive Theil–Sen slope is median[(vⱼ−vᵢ)/(tⱼ−tᵢ)] over unequal time pairs; Kendall's tau describes monotonic association. ISO dates give slope units per 365.25-day year, while numeric time retains its source unit. No serial-independence significance tests or forecasts are added. The left panel is a histogram of well means with the equal-well estimate and its bootstrap interval. The right shows the distribution of available slopes, or measurement counts per well when slopes are unavailable. Full counts, trends and bootstrap summaries are exported.

Chapter 8: Water-quality indices — WQI and DWQI

The WQI & DWQI module calculates indices from a selected set of chemical parameters, objectives and weights, then displays the results in 26 views. These comprise 12 WQI views, 12 DWQI views, a spatial comparison and a WQI versus DWQI scatter. The calculation must be reviewed before the map or category is interpreted: the name of an index does not by itself identify its parameter set, standards edition or classification rule.

8.1 Scientific basis of the index calculation

A water-quality index aggregates selected chemical information into one score. Its meaning depends on the parameter set, objectives, rating functions and weights. Brown et al. (1970) provide historical index context, while Ramakrishnaiah et al. (2009) describe a relative-weight groundwater formulation. AquaProv supports configurable formulations; the method name alone is insufficient to reproduce a score.

For a concentration Ci and positive upper objective Si, the ratio rating is qi = 100Ci/Si. An ideal-value rating instead uses a declared ideal Ii. The aggregate uses normalized weights Wi:

qi = 100(Ci − Ii)/(Si − Ii);   Index = ΣiWiqi
Inverse-standard option: Wi = (1/Si)/Σj(1/Sj)
Relative-weight option: Wi = wi/Σjwj (8.1)

The option labelled Brown family implements the inverse-standard weighted-arithmetic convention above; it does not reproduce the original NSF nine-parameter rating curves. The relative-weight and DWQI options use the supplied importance weights, adapting the formulation of Ramakrishnaiah et al. (2009). DWQI is not a uniquely standardized equation. Export the exact parameter configuration when citing either result.

Illustrative calculation. Take two synthetic parameters with C = (5, 10), S = (10, 20), zero ideals and ratio ratings. Both ratings are 50. Inverse-standard weights are 2/3 and 1/3, giving an index of 50; equal importance weights also give 50. If the second concentration increases to 20, its rating becomes 100 and the two scores become 66.67 and 75.00 respectively. Thus a change in weighting can alter the score without any change in the input dataset.

All selected values must be expressed in their declared canonical objective units before weighting. This is particularly important for inverse-standard weights, which depend on the numerical objectives. For parameters with lower and upper limits, the explicit two-sided rating measures departure on either side of an ideal. A signed one-sided ideal rating can produce negative values; inspect the per-parameter compliance table rather than assuming the aggregate captures every failure.

8.1.1 CCME scope, frequency and amplitude

The CCME WQI summarizes repeated objective attainment through scope F1, frequency F2 and amplitude F3 (Canadian Council of Ministers of the Environment [CCME], 2001). Scope is the percentage of selected variables failing at least once; frequency is the percentage of valid tests that fail. For an upper objective, a failed-test excursion is C/S−1; for a positive lower objective it is S/C−1. Let nse be the sum of excursions divided by the number of valid tests:

F3 = 100nse/(nse+1);   CCME WQI = 100 − √[(F12 + F22 + F32)/3] (8.2)

Higher CCME scores indicate greater objective attainment, whereas the arithmetic formulations use lower scores for better categories. AquaProv requires at least four selected variables and four distinct measured occasions per variable in each site/period group; missing tests do not count as passes. This implements the documented minimum convention. The CCME (2017) update explains that greater parameter coverage, preferably at least eight parameters, generally gives more consistent results. Passing the software minimum is therefore not a claim that a monitoring design is sufficient.

Use consistent variables, objectives, periods and sampling coverage for comparisons. The CCME method does not use arithmetic importance weights or ideal values. Record its own classification scheme and sample support separately from WQI/DWQI arithmetic results (CCME, 2017).

8.1.2 Objectives, missingness and scientific reporting

A guideline source and an index method have different roles: the former supplies objectives and context, while the latter defines aggregation. Where selected drinking-water values are taken from World Health Organization (2022), retain the edition, chemical species and any qualifications. Custom or unverified objectives must be identified as such. A composite category cannot establish compliance for an omitted contaminant.

AquaProv retains incomplete arithmetic-index samples as unassessed and does not redistribute their missing weights. Parameter-compliance percentages use each parameter's measured denominator, which may differ from the complete-score denominator. The worked Sukinda example has 47 source rows and 45 complete composite scores. Report both numbers so that a mapped or classified subset is not mistaken for the full sampling record.

8.6 CCME repeated-test assessment

CCME is calculated once per site and assessment period, not once per individual chemical sample. Choose the site, sampling-occasion and optional season/period columns. Distinct occasions are required. Duplicate site/period/occasion records must be resolved explicitly. AquaProv conservatively requires at least four variables with at least four measured tests each in every group; insufficient groups remain Unassessed. The CCME user manual explains the original 2001 minimum and the advantages of more extensive monitoring.

  • F1: 100 × variables failing at least once / selected variables.
  • F2: 100 × failed tests / measured tests.
  • Upper-objective excursion: C/S−1; lower-objective excursion: S/C−1.
  • nse: sum of excursions / measured tests, including passing tests in the denominator.
  • F3: 100 nse/(nse+1).

A zero measurement failing a positive minimum has infinite excursion, so its amplitude takes the limiting value 100. A failed zero-tolerance upper objective cannot be ratio-normalized and is rejected. Arithmetic weights and ideal values do not enter CCME. Spatial CCME plots require a stable coordinate for all visits belonging to a site/period. Seasonal CCME maps require enough repeat observations in each season.

CCME categories describe attainment of the chosen objectives, often for aquatic ecosystem assessment. They are never relabelled as drinking-water safety classifications. Select objectives appropriate to the intended use.

8.8 Missing data, maps and comparisons

Arithmetic indices require every selected parameter for that sample. Missing values produce Unassessed indices; weights are never silently renormalized. Non-numeric values, censoring qualifiers, infinities and physically invalid negative concentrations must be resolved before calculation. Compliance percentages use the measured denominator for each parameter, with assessed and missing counts shown; missing results are not counted as passing.

IDW and ordinary kriging operate on continuous calculated indices. Classification zones are assigned to the predicted grid afterward. The default mask is the sampled convex hull; an imported/drawn study boundary can clip it or explicitly enable extrapolation. Extrapolated cells and kriging prediction variance are exported. A boundary is not a groundwater-flow barrier. Kriging may predict outside the observed index range; AquaProv reports the values without hidden clipping.

Seasonal maps use a common coordinate origin, equal map extents and common colour limits. Their sampled hulls may differ. Grouped seasonal bars show a mean per site/season, with counts and SDs exported; missing combinations are gaps. Box plots and violins use available index records and report sample counts. These are descriptive comparisons, not paired significance tests. Constant or singleton data are shown as observations in the violin view because a density cannot be estimated.

WQI/DWQI comparison maps use separate numerical scales and retain both calculation reports. In particular, CCME's higher-is-better score cannot be compared numerically as if it had the same meaning as an arithmetic DWQI.

8.12 Mathematical background of the plots

The following sections explain how the inputs become plotted coordinates, summaries or predictions. Each section names the views that use it. Shared formulas are stated once within this chapter; follow the applicable preprocessing and calculation sections together. Examples labelled illustrative are arithmetic checks, not findings from the field datasets.

8.12.1 Calculate the index before drawing the plot

Applies to: all plots in this chapter.

For each sample and selected parameter i, first convert concentration Cᵢ into the canonical unit and named chemical basis of its standard. Let Iᵢ be the ideal, Sᵢ the rating standard, and Wᵢ the normalized weight. The arithmetic index is (Brown et al., 1970; Ramakrishnaiah et al., 2009):

Index = ∑ᵢWᵢqᵢ;   ∑ᵢWᵢ = 1
Ratio rating: qᵢ = 100Cᵢ/Sᵢ
Ideal rating: qᵢ = 100(Cᵢ − Iᵢ)/(Sᵢ − Iᵢ) (8.3)

For a two-sided rating with Lᵢ < Iᵢ < Uᵢ, use qᵢ = 100(Cᵢ−Iᵢ)/(Uᵢ−Iᵢ) above the ideal, and qᵢ = 100(Iᵢ−Cᵢ)/(Iᵢ−Lᵢ) below it. This differs from the signed ideal formula, which may produce negative ratings. An ideal of zero makes the upper-standard ideal rating equal to the ratio rating. Individual ratings and contributions Wᵢqᵢ are exported.

Selected method Normalized weights and meaning
Weighted arithmetic WQI (inverse-standard weights) Wᵢ = (1/Sᵢ)/∑ⱼ(1/Sⱼ), using canonical standard units
Relative-weight groundwater WQI Wᵢ = wᵢ/∑ⱼwⱼ from the chosen positive importance weights
Weighted DWQI The same weighted-sum form with independently chosen DWQI parameters, ratings and importance weights
Custom weighted DWQI The explicitly configured weights, ratings, limits and classification define the calculation

The weighted arithmetic option implements this inverse-standard convention; it does not reproduce the original NSF nine-parameter rating curves. Inverse-standard weights depend on the declared canonical units, so input and standard unit conversion must precede weighting. DWQI has no single universal formula. A method name alone is insufficient to reproduce a result.

Illustrative calculation: with ratio ratings C = (10, 0.75) mg/L and S = (50, 1.5) mg/L, q = (20, 50). Inverse-standard weights are (3/103, 100/103), giving WQI ≈ 49.126. Equal importance weights instead give 35. This is a two-parameter arithmetic illustration, not a validated drinking-water assessment or CCME dataset.

An arithmetic score requires every selected measurement in the row. If any are missing, the score remains unassessed; weights are not renormalized to the available subset. A favourable composite can coexist with an individual exceedance. Review selected hazards, standards edition, per-parameter compliance and missing counts.

8.12.2 Alternative WQI: CCME scope, frequency and amplitude

Applies to: all WQI views, including WQI in spatial comparison and the WQI versus DWQI scatter, when CCME is selected.

If CCME is selected, AquaProv calculates one WQI per site/period from repeated occasions rather than one arithmetic score per row. It requires at least four selected variables and at least four measured occasions per variable in each site/period. Duplicate site/period/occasion records must be resolved explicitly (Canadian Council of Ministers of the Environment [CCME], 2001; CCME, 2017).

F₁ = 100 × variables failing at least once / selected variables
F₂ = 100 × failed tests / measured tests
Upper-limit excursion = C/U − 1 when C > U
Lower-limit excursion = L/C − 1 when C < L
nse = ∑ excursions / measured tests
F₃ = 100 nse/(1+nse)
CCME WQI = 100 − √[(F₁² + F₂² + F₃²)/3] (8.4)

Passing tests contribute zero excursion. Missing tests contribute neither passes nor failures. A zero failing a positive minimum has infinite excursion and the limiting F₃ = 100. An exceeded zero upper limit cannot be ratio-normalized and is rejected. Groups with insufficient tests remain unassessed. Higher CCME values indicate better attainment, the opposite numerical direction from arithmetic WQI/DWQI. CCME does not use arithmetic importance weights or ideals.

The supplied WQI/DWQI worked figures use arithmetic methods; this alternative explains how the calculation changes when the method selector changes. CCME categories assess attainment of selected objectives, not complete drinking-water safety.

8.12.3 Turn index values into classes

Applies to: all plots in this chapter.

Classification is applied to the full-precision score after calculation. Default arithmetic WQI cuts are 25, 50, 75 and 100; default relative-weight WQI/DWQI cuts are 50, 100, 200 and 300. Both use the labels Excellent, Good, Poor, Very Poor and Unsuitable in that order, and both include an exact cut in the lower-valued class. Thus arithmetic WQI = 25 is Excellent, while 25.01 is Good.

CCME uses cuts 45, 65, 80 and 95 with labels Poor, Marginal, Fair, Good and Excellent. Exact cuts enter the next higher class; 95 is Excellent. Custom schemes can change these thresholds, colours and inclusion rules. Nonfinite scores are Unassessed. Always interpret the exported classification scheme rather than assuming colours imply universal thresholds.

8.12.4 Interpolate calculated scores

Applies to: WQI and DWQI spatial distribution, 3D surface, contour map, classification zones and seasonal comparison maps; WQI and DWQI spatial comparison.

The mapping pipeline is chemistry → sample or site-period index → retained coordinates → interpolation. IDW or ordinary kriging operates on the numeric index values, not on class numbers. This generally differs from interpolating every concentration and then recalculating the index, especially with missing inputs, different weights or nonlinear methods. The worked geographic figures use IDW power 2 and a 120 × 120 grid with full-boundary coverage enabled. Sukinda uses its supplied KML; the separate Liu seasonal study uses a labelled sampling envelope. Coordinate exclusions and extrapolated cells are reported separately from chemical exclusions.

Only finite scores with valid coordinates enter the spatial field. A blank map area is outside the selected support or unassessed; it is not index zero. For CCME, all rows belonging to a site-period must agree on location. Co-located independent records need an explicit resolution before interpolation; the map does not silently average duplicate coordinates.

8.12.5 Coordinates used for distances

Applies to: WQI and DWQI spatial distribution, 3D surface, contour map, classification zones and seasonal comparison maps; WQI and DWQI spatial comparison.

Geographic location and distance are separate operations. Projected input uses its source distance units. For WGS84 longitude / latitude → local metres, AquaProv converts degrees to a local metric approximation about the mean longitude λ₀ and latitude φ₀:

x = R(λ − λ₀)cos φ₀;   y = R(φ − φ₀)
R = 6,371,008.8 m; angles in radians
dᵢⱼ = √[(xᵢ − xⱼ)² + (yᵢ − yⱼ)²] (8.5)

The local approximation is restricted to extents no greater than 3° in either direction and latitudes within ±85°. For larger or more precise studies, choose a suitable projected CRS and consistent distance units. Changing the basemap for display does not change the analysis distance coordinates. Duplicate locations and fewer than three non-collinear observations cannot define these interpolated surfaces without deliberate data preparation.

The current Distance coordinates control also offers automatic UTM/UPS and a custom projected CRS in metres. These transform WGS84 positions through the selected projection before calculating distances; they do not use the local equation above. Enter Analysis CRS for the custom mode, and retain the selected CRS in the calculation record. For existing projected X/Y, Source CRS override declares the original projected system.

Choose Grid definition → Cells per axis for 20–1,000 cells per axis, or Cell size in analysis units for explicit spacing in the analysis coordinate units. The cell-size option has a 1,000,000-point limit. Resolution changes where predictions are evaluated; it does not increase sample support. Colour classification and Surface drawing change the presentation. Retain exported class boundaries when comparing Equal intervals, Quantiles, Natural Breaks (Jenks) or custom classes; large-grid Natural Breaks uses a bounded histogram approximation.

8.12.6 Inverse-distance weighting (IDW)

Applies to: WQI and DWQI spatial distribution, 3D surface, contour map, classification zones and seasonal comparison maps; WQI and DWQI spatial comparison.

At target s₀, IDW calculates a normalized weighted average of the selected neighbouring sample values vᵢ. Nearest neighbours → All samples includes all eligible observations; a positive count selects the nearest available observations and includes ties at the cutoff distance. With distance dᵢ = ‖s₀ − sᵢ‖ and positive power p, the sums run over that selected set (Shepard, 1968):

wᵢ = dᵢ−p / ∑ⱼdⱼ−p
v̂(s₀) = ∑ᵢwᵢvᵢ (8.6)

If the target exactly matches a sample, its observed value is returned. Internally distances are normalized by the nearest distance before exponentiation for numerical stability; normalized weights are unchanged. All weights are nonnegative and sum to 1, so predictions stay within the sample value range. Increasing p favours nearby samples more strongly. At distances 1 and 2 with p = 2, illustrative values 10 and 30 yield (10 + 30/4)/(1 + 1/4) = 14.

The grid is restricted to the convex hull, and to the study-boundary intersection when selected, unless boundary extrapolation is explicitly enabled. Grid resolution changes evaluation density, not the number of observations. IDW supplies no prediction variance or probability. Boundaries clip the display; they are not physical groundwater-flow barriers.

8.12.7 Ordinary kriging: model, weights and variance

Applies to: WQI and DWQI spatial distribution, 3D surface, contour map, classification zones and seasonal comparison maps; WQI and DWQI spatial comparison.

Ordinary kriging assumes an unknown locally constant mean and an isotropic covariance depending only on distance. Let a be range, s the structured partial sill, and n₀ the nugget, all in consistent distance or squared measurement units. With r = d/a (Cressie, 1993):

Spherical C(d) = s(1 − 1.5r + 0.5r³) for r < 1; otherwise 0
Exponential C(d) = s exp(−3r) (8.7)

For the exponential model, a is the practical range where structured covariance is s exp(−3), about 5% of s. Form Kᵢⱼ = C(dᵢⱼ) + n₀δᵢⱼ, cᵢ = C(‖sᵢ−s₀‖), and the ones vector 1. The unscaled equivalent of AquaProv's linear system is:

K w + 1μ = c;   1ᵀw = 1
v̂(s₀) = wᵀv
σ²pred(s₀) = s + n₀ − wᵀc − μ (8.8)

μ is the Lagrange multiplier under this covariance sign convention. The implementation scales covariances by s+n₀ to improve numerical conditioning. The sum-to-one constraint preserves a constant mean. Weights may be negative, so predictions need not stay within observed values. The reported variance is for a new observation, including the nugget; with a nonzero nugget, a coincident target is not forced to reproduce an observed sample exactly.

The Maps module uses user-supplied range, partial sill and nugget and does not fit a variogram automatically. Fitted models are provided separately in Assessment. Neither module silently estimates anisotropy or removes a trend. Validate the model with held-out data before using its interpolated pattern scientifically.

8.12.8 Spatial-distribution view

Applies to: WQI · 2D spatial distribution; DWQI · 2D spatial distribution.

The continuous colour field displays the interpolated index v̂(x,y); sample markers provide observed spatial support. Colour changes represent numeric score differences under the selected method and scale. Neither a smooth field nor proximity to a sample proves that the unsampled water has the same chemistry. Inspect the exported grid, mask, units and interpolation settings with the original sample values.

8.12.9 Three-dimensional index surface

Applies to: WQI · 3D surface; DWQI · 3D surface.

The horizontal coordinates are the map's distance coordinates and the height is the interpolated index, not elevation or aquifer depth. Height and colour encode the same calculated field. Camera, opacity and mesh controls alter presentation, while the exported grid keeps the numerical surface. This WQI/DWQI surface uses the chosen IDW/kriging engine; it is distinct from the generic piecewise-linear XYZ surface in the 3D module.

8.12.10 Contour view

Applies to: WQI · Contour map; DWQI · Contour map.

A contour at level ℓ is the set {(x,y): v̂(x,y) = ℓ} traced from the calculated grid. Filled bands cover successive numeric ranges. Contour spacing depends on both the interpolator and selected levels; it is not a groundwater hydraulic gradient or evidence of a transport direction. Values outside the mask remain unassessed even when the renderer uses a support grid to clip contours cleanly at the boundary.

8.12.11 Classification zones

Applies to: WQI · Classification zones; DWQI · Classification zones.

Each finite interpolated score is passed to the selected index classification rule. The map therefore shows classes of an interpolated numeric index. It does not interpolate categorical labels or calculate probabilities of belonging to a class. Sharp colour boundaries are threshold crossings of a smooth field and can shift with the index method, threshold settings and interpolation model. Unassessed cells remain outside the classification.

8.12.12 Sampling points and values

Applies to: WQI · Sampling points and values; DWQI · Sampling points and values.

This route draws the calculated index at each retained sample/site-period coordinate. It does not create a spatial grid. Marker colour and labels identify the score and its classification under the chosen method. Empty space between points has no predicted value. Validate coordinate mapping and inspect unassessed samples before interpreting apparent spatial clusters.

8.12.13 Histogram counts and bin edges

Applies to: WQI · Histogram; DWQI · Histogram.

Let vᵢ be each finite retained index. For edges b₀,…,bₖ, bar j counts vᵢ in [bⱼ,bⱼ₊₁), with the final interval including its right edge. Bar heights are counts, not probability densities:

Nⱼ = ∑ᵢ1[bⱼ ≤ vᵢ < bⱼ₊₁];   ∑ⱼNⱼ = number of retained scores (8.9)

The final-bin inclusion is the exception shown above. The configured bin count or NumPy/Matplotlib auto rule determines edges, which are exported with counts. Missing composite scores are excluded, so the histogram total may be smaller than the input row count. A CCME observation is a site-period score. Bin choices can change apparent modes; they do not change the underlying index calculation.

8.12.14 Quartiles, whiskers and individual extremes

Applies to: WQI · Box plot; WQI · Seasonal box plots; DWQI · Box plot; DWQI · Seasonal box plots.

For a set of finite plotted values v, let Q₁, Q₂ and Q₃ be the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles. AquaProv's NumPy/Matplotlib path uses linear quantiles. If sorted values are v₍₀₎…v₍ₙ₋₁₎, a quantile q lies at index (n−1)q and linearly interpolates neighbouring values.

IQR = Q₃ − Q₁
Lower fence = Q₁ − 1.5 IQR;   upper fence = Q₃ + 1.5 IQR (8.10)

The box spans Q₁ to Q₃ with a median line. Whiskers reach the most extreme observations within the fences, not necessarily the fence coordinates. Values beyond the whiskers remain observations; the plot does not remove them. For values 1, 2, 3, 4, 9, Q₁ = 2, median = 3, Q₃ = 4, and the upper fence is 7, so 9 is beyond the whisker. This descriptive range is not a confidence interval.

8.12.15 The index box plot

Applies to: WQI · Box plot; DWQI · Box plot.

The box uses finite WQI or DWQI scores in their original index scale, without z-standardization. Fliers beyond the whiskers remain visible, and distribution_summary and box_outliers record the statistics and source records. These are index outliers under a descriptive rule, not automatically invalid analyses or threshold exceedances. The sample count excludes unassessed composite scores.

8.12.16 Kernel-density shape of a distribution

Applies to: WQI · Violin plot; DWQI · Violin plot.

A violin mirrors a one-dimensional Gaussian kernel density along its numeric axis. For n finite values vᵢ and bandwidth h:

f̂(v) = [1/(nh√(2π))] ∑ᵢexp[−(v−vᵢ)²/(2h²)] (8.11)

Matplotlib's default violin estimator uses Scott bandwidth (sample SD × n−1/5 in one dimension), evaluated over the observed range. Width is scaled for display and does not directly encode sample count. The median and extrema are shown. A smooth outline is an estimated distribution shape, not evidence of a continuous physical process or a confidence interval. Very small or constant samples cannot support a meaningful density; the applicable module falls back to observed points as described below.

8.12.17 The index violin plot

Applies to: WQI · Violin plot; DWQI · Violin plot.

The density uses finite composite scores with no z-standardization. This module requires more than one value and nonzero range for a violin; a singleton or constant sample is shown as observed points. The exported quartiles and outlier table use the same retained scores. Density width and index-class thresholds describe different things: a wide region indicates many similar scores, not better or worse quality by itself.

8.12.18 Separate seasonal fields on a common spatial basis

Applies to: WQI · Seasonal comparison maps; DWQI · Seasonal comparison maps.

Scores are split by the selected season/period and each field is interpolated separately. A common coordinate origin and a shared numeric scale for the same index make panels comparable. Each panel still depends on its own retained scores, sample geometry and mask; differences can reflect changed sampling coverage as well as changed chemistry. The map does not estimate a paired seasonal effect or significance. Match site identifiers, standards, methods and period definitions before interpreting the comparison.

8.12.19 Arithmetic means of index records within site and season

Applies to: WQI · Seasonal grouped bars; DWQI · Seasonal grouped bars.

For site s, season t and nst available scores, each bar is:

Īst = (1/nst)∑r in site s, season tIᵣ (8.12)

AquaProv calculates each record's index before averaging. The exported seasonal_site_statistics contains means, counts and sample SDs; bars themselves do not show an inferred confidence interval. A missing site–season combination is a gap, not zero. Seasonal summary tables also give mean, median and sample SD across available records. These descriptive comparisons do not account for unequal repeats or establish a paired seasonal effect.

8.12.20 Seasonal boxes pool available scores

Applies to: WQI · Seasonal box plots; DWQI · Seasonal box plots.

Each selected season contributes all its finite sample/site-period scores to one box. These are raw index values, not standardized values and not first averaged to equal-weight site means. Whiskers and fliers follow the quartile rule above; summary tables give each season's count, mean, median and sample SD. A well sampled repeatedly can contribute repeatedly. Different well coverage or sample counts can shift a box even without a within-well seasonal change.

8.12.21 Parameter compliance uses its own measured denominator

Applies to: WQI · Parameter compliance; DWQI · Parameter compliance.

For each parameter i, a finite measurement meets the selected limits when C ≥ L (if a lower limit exists) and C ≤ U (if an upper limit exists). Equality at either limit is compliant. Let nᵢ count measured tests and fᵢ count failures:

Outside limits (%) = 100fᵢ/nᵢ
Meets limits (%) = 100(nᵢ − fᵢ)/nᵢ (8.13)

The horizontal stacked bars sum to 100% for each assessed parameter. Missing counts are separate; nᵢ can differ between parameters and can exceed the number of complete composite indices. If nᵢ = 0, the percentage is undefined. For 45 measured tests with 9 failures, the bars are 20% outside and 80% meeting limits, regardless of other parameters' missing records. This view tests individual concentrations, not whether a WQI/DWQI score passes a class threshold.

8.12.22 WQI and DWQI side-by-side maps

Applies to: WQI and DWQI spatial comparison.

WQI and DWQI are calculated independently with their selected methods, weights and classification schemes, then mapped independently. Their numerical colour scales remain separate. A value of 50 need not mean the same class, parameter contribution or attainment in both panels. Spatial differences can arise from weighting choices alone. Compare exported per-parameter contributions and schemes before interpreting them as a physical change in water quality; there is no subtraction, averaging or conversion between the two indices in this view.

8.12.23 WQI and DWQI paired by source observation

Applies to: WQI versus DWQI scatter.

AquaProv calculates WQI and DWQI with their independently reviewed parameter profiles, then joins the results by the original source-row identifier. Each plotted point is:

(xᵣ, yᵣ) = (WQIᵣ, DWQIᵣ), where both indices are finite (8.14)

An identifier repeated on different source rows does not merge the observations. Rows missing either index are excluded from the paired scatter, with the underlying index results retained for review. The index_comparison result table records source row, sample, paired values and optional highlighting.

The optional identity line is y = x. Its limits control the drawn segment; the line is not a regression or a test of agreement. WQI and DWQI may have different weights, scales and classifications, so distance from the line does not validate either method. For example, a point at (80, 120) simply records those two calculated index values for one observation.

The worked Sukinda example retains 45 finite pairs from 47 input rows. It uses inverse-standard weighted arithmetic WQI and equal-importance weighted DWQI, zero ideals and the stated WHO 2026 objectives for NO3, F, total Cr, Ni and Mn. Optional threshold highlighting compares a separately selected source column with a stated threshold in that column's units; it does not change the calculated indices.

Chapter 9: PHREEQC mineral saturation

The Mineral SI module examines saturation indices for selected minerals. It can read existing SI results or calculate initial aqueous speciation from measured chemistry using PHREEQC. Its eight views summarize samples and minerals, compare distributions and pairs, and map observed or interpolated SI. The underlying calculation is thermodynamic: a positive saturation index does not establish that a mineral is present or that precipitation will occur at a measurable rate.

9.1 Thermodynamic basis and the role of PHREEQC

A mineral saturation index compares an ion activity product with the equilibrium constant for a specified dissolution reaction. PHREEQC computes aqueous speciation and activities from the mapped solution composition and the selected thermodynamic database (Parkhurst & Appelo, 2013). AquaProv calls the IPhreeqc library through its program interface (Charlton & Parkhurst, 2011); it does not approximate the index by multiplying reported total concentrations.

IAP = ∏jajνj;   SI = log10[IAP/K(T)]
aj = γjmj on the database's activity standard-state basis (9.1)

Here aj is species activity, γj its activity coefficient, mj its molality, νj its reaction exponent and K(T) the equilibrium constant at solution temperature. For calcite dissolution, IAP = aCa2+aCO3²−; free carbonate activity is not equal to a reported bicarbonate or total-inorganic-carbon concentration. The actual phase reaction and activity model come from the chosen database (Parkhurst & Appelo, 2013).

Illustrative interpretation. IAP/K values of 0.1, 1 and 10 correspond to SI values of −1, 0 and +1. Thus one SI unit represents a tenfold change in the saturation ratio. Undersaturation permits dissolution if a mineral is present and accessible; supersaturation indicates a thermodynamic tendency that may be limited by kinetics or nucleation. SI does not measure mineral abundance, a reaction rate or the amount that will precipitate.

9.1.1 Input conditions and equilibrium assumptions

pH constrains hydrogen activity, while alkalinity or a mapped carbon quantity constrains carbonate chemistry. Temperature affects equilibrium constants and speciation; density connects volume-based concentrations with solvent-mass units. The selected database determines complexes and activity coefficients, so it is part of the scientific model and should be recorded by name and hash (Parkhurst & Appelo, 2013).

AquaProv's present calculation performs initial aqueous speciation at the PHREEQC default pressure of 1 atm. Selecting a mineral requests its SI; it does not equilibrate the solution with that mineral. No measured ion is automatically adjusted to force electroneutrality. The worked example uses phreeqc.dat, 25 °C, density 1 kg/L and the declared TA-as-CaCO3 basis. Treat those solution settings as recorded assumptions wherever measured values are unavailable.

Redox-sensitive calculations additionally depend on valence-specific inputs or a defensible redox specification. A total-iron concentration does not establish Fe(III). When measured or assumed redox is absent, AquaProv records the PHREEQC pe default and leaves affected iron-oxide indices unassessed unless the required Fe(III) input is available. Review the solution audit and required components for each mineral before comparing counts across minerals.

9.1.2 Imported SI and mapped SI

Imported SI values retain the upstream calculation and its limitations. Record the source software, database, solution conditions and rounding precision when available; these cannot be reconstructed from an SI column. Undefined PHREEQC sentinel values −999 and −999.999 are treated as unassessed. The display's exact-zero equilibrium class should not be interpreted as a measurement-tolerance band.

Interpolating SI predicts a logarithmic field from the sampled indices, rather than recalculating equilibrium at unsampled locations. For example, an arithmetic midpoint between SI = −1 and SI = +1 is zero, corresponding to the geometric midpoint of the two saturation ratios. Spatial prediction variance excludes the chemistry and database uncertainty of the upstream PHREEQC calculation. Report those two sources of uncertainty separately (Cressie, 1993; Parkhurst & Appelo, 2013).

9.8 Scientific definition and results

PHREEQC calculates SI = log10(IAP / K(T)) from species activities and the temperature-dependent equilibrium constant of the chosen phase. Water activity participates where the database reaction requires it. Concentration products are never used as an SI approximation.

  • SI < 0: undersaturated; dissolution is thermodynamically possible if the mineral is present and accessible.
  • SI = 0: at equilibrium at the precision of the result.
  • SI > 0: supersaturated; precipitation is thermodynamically possible, but kinetics/nucleation may inhibit it.

No rounding or hidden equilibrium band changes these classes. SI is neither a reaction rate nor evidence of mineral presence. The engine speciates the initial solutions; it does not equilibrate them with minerals or adjust an ion to force charge balance.

Results & methods contains individual SI values and statuses; counts, mean, median, sample SD, minimum, maximum and quartiles by mineral; class counts/percentages; optional group summaries; scientific interpretations; and source provenance. Percentages exclude unassessed values and expose their count separately. SD is undefined for a single observation. Repeated/spatial observations are not assumed independent for inference.

Calculated results additionally include pH, pe, temperature, density, ionic strength, PHREEQC charge-balance percent error, log activities (including H2O), phase log K, reconstructed log IAP, exact PHREEQC input, full database text and engine/database hashes. In Results & methods → diagnostics, Charge balance error (%) shows each sample's signed numerical percentage at the returned precision. The final column, charge_balance_review, also shows Above 5% absolute error or Within 5% absolute error; exactly +5% or -5% is within the threshold. Excluded samples have a blank numeric value and an Unassessed review. Both columns are exported. An absolute charge-balance error above 5% remains a review warning, not a correction. Native-engine failure cancels the calculation without issuing partial SI results.

9.13 Mathematical background of the plots

The following sections explain how the inputs become plotted coordinates, summaries or predictions. Each section names the views that use it. Shared formulas are stated once within this chapter; follow the applicable preprocessing and calculation sections together. Examples labelled illustrative are arithmetic checks, not findings from the field datasets.

9.13.1 Thermodynamic definition of mineral saturation index

Applies to: all plots in this chapter.

For the mineral dissolution reaction written in the selected PHREEQC database, species activities determine the ion activity product (IAP). Let νⱼ be reaction stoichiometric exponents and K(T) its equilibrium constant at the solution temperature (Parkhurst & Appelo, 2013):

IAP = ∏ⱼaⱼνⱼ
SI = log₁₀[IAP/K(T)] = ∑ⱼνⱼlog₁₀aⱼ − log₁₀K(T)
aⱼ = γⱼmⱼ (on the database's activity standard-state basis) (9.2)

Here mⱼ is species molality and γⱼ the activity coefficient; water activity enters where required by the database reaction. For calcite dissolution CaCO₃(s) ⇌ Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻, IAP = aCa²⁺aCO₃²⁻. Total calcium and total inorganic carbon are not automatically the free-species activities. For dolomite, the analogous product is aCa²⁺aMg²⁺aCO₃²⁻². PHREEQC uses each phase's actual database reaction; AquaProv does not hard-code an approximate concentration product.

Illustrative check: IAP/K = 0.1 gives SI = −1; a ratio of 1 gives SI = 0; a ratio of 10 gives SI = +1. One SI unit is a tenfold change in the saturation ratio. Negative SI permits dissolution if the mineral is present and accessible; positive SI indicates thermodynamic precipitation potential, which may be inhibited by kinetics or nucleation. SI is not mineral abundance or a reaction rate.

9.13.2 Background calculation: aqueous speciation and activities

Applies to: all plots in this chapter.

In calculation mode AquaProv writes the mapped elemental totals, measured pH and solution conditions to IPhreeqc. PHREEQC solves aqueous mass-action and component-balance equations under those inputs; the selected database controls complexes, equilibrium constants and the activity model. Ionic strength, I = ½∑ⱼmⱼzⱼ², links the full dissolved composition to activity coefficients. Thus omitting major ions can affect the SI of a mineral even when its own components are measured (Parkhurst & Appelo, 2013; Charlton & Parkhurst, 2011).

pH constrains hydrogen activity by pH = −log₁₀aH⁺. Alkalinity or DIC constrains carbonate chemistry; bicarbonate concentration is not silently treated as a titrated alkalinity. Density relates volume-based inputs to solvent-mass concentrations. Temperature changes equilibrium constants and species distributions. For an explicitly supplied Eh versus the standard hydrogen electrode, pe = F·Eh/(ln(10)RT), with Eh in volts and T in kelvin. Missing redox information cannot be inferred merely from a total-iron concentration.

The workflow speciates initial solutions at 1 atm. It neither equilibrates the water with selected minerals nor adjusts an ion to force charge balance. The worked dataset uses phreeqc.dat, 25 °C, density 1 kg/L and TA as CaCO₃; these are stated assumptions, not all measured properties. Review Mineral SI inputs and reporting bases before changing databases or using saline/redox-sensitive water.

In import mode, the supplied SI values are preserved without repeating this calculation. A database/version, source rounding and activity model cannot be recovered from the SI column alone. Undefined PHREEQC sentinels −999.999 and −999 become unassessed, not extreme undersaturation.

9.13.3 Exact classes and mineral-specific sample counts

Applies to: all plots in this chapter.

A finite SI < 0 is Undersaturated; SI = 0 is At equilibrium; SI > 0 is Supersaturated. The classifier uses full numerical values with no hidden tolerance or rounding. A label rounded to 0.00 may therefore still be negative or positive. A zero in imported data may also reflect rounding by its source.

Per-mineral summaries use finite SI values to calculate mean, median, sample SD (denominator n−1), quartiles and extremes. Class percentage = 100 × class count / that mineral's assessed count. Missing SI is excluded from this denominator and reported separately. For 8 assessed values with 6 supersaturated and 2 undersaturated, supersaturation is 75%, even if two other source records were unassessed. Comparing minerals requires checking their potentially different assessed counts.

9.13.4 SI bars by sample and mineral

Applies to: SI bar plot.

Each bar height is one sample/event's SI for a selected mineral. Bars extend above or below the zero reference; exact zeros are marked on that line. These are log saturation ratios, not concentrations or percentages. The sample–mineral matrix must have unique event identities; duplicate pairs require grouping or explicit preparation. Missing entries do not become zero-height equilibrium bars. The worked bar figure selects 12 samples for readability.

9.13.5 SI heatmap cells

Applies to: SI heatmap.

Each cell is the original SI for a sample/event and mineral. A zero-centered diverging scale separates negative and positive values; no row or column z-standardization is applied. Numerical labels may be rounded, while colouring and classification use the underlying value. Grey/NA cells are unassessed. Differences between adjacent rows do not themselves indicate a spatial or temporal gradient; row order follows the selected records.

9.13.6 Quartiles, whiskers and individual extremes

Applies to: SI box plot.

For a set of finite plotted values v, let Q₁, Q₂ and Q₃ be the 25th, 50th and 75th percentiles. AquaProv's NumPy/Matplotlib path uses linear quantiles. If sorted values are v₍₀₎…v₍ₙ₋₁₎, a quantile q lies at index (n−1)q and linearly interpolates neighbouring values.

IQR = Q₃ − Q₁
Lower fence = Q₁ − 1.5 IQR;   upper fence = Q₃ + 1.5 IQR (9.3)

The box spans Q₁ to Q₃ with a median line. Whiskers reach the most extreme observations within the fences, not necessarily the fence coordinates. Values beyond the whiskers remain observations; the plot does not remove them. For values 1, 2, 3, 4, 9, Q₁ = 2, median = 3, Q₃ = 4, and the upper fence is 7, so 9 is beyond the whisker. This descriptive range is not a confidence interval.

9.13.7 SI boxes with all observed points

Applies to: SI box plot.

Each mineral's box uses its finite, unstandardized SI values. Separate boxplot fliers are hidden because AquaProv overlays all observations, coloured by their exact saturation class. Horizontal jitter separates overlapping markers and does not alter SI. The box follows the usual 1.5-IQR rule; the points retain extremes beyond it. A median SI above zero describes the retained observations and is not a confidence statement about the entire aquifer.

9.13.8 Kernel-density shape of a distribution

Applies to: SI violin plot.

A violin mirrors a one-dimensional Gaussian kernel density along its numeric axis. For n finite values vᵢ and bandwidth h:

f̂(v) = [1/(nh√(2π))] ∑ᵢexp[−(v−vᵢ)²/(2h²)] (9.4)

Matplotlib's default violin estimator uses Scott bandwidth (sample SD × n−1/5 in one dimension), evaluated over the observed range. Width is scaled for display and does not directly encode sample count. The median and extrema are shown. A smooth outline is an estimated distribution shape, not evidence of a continuous physical process or a confidence interval. Very small or constant samples cannot support a meaningful density; the applicable module falls back to observed points as described below.

9.13.9 SI violin with observed sample support

Applies to: SI violin plot.

Each mineral needs at least three finite, nonconstant values for the density outline. Otherwise AquaProv shows only observed points. Medians and extrema accompany the violin, and every retained point is overlaid with horizontal display jitter and an unchanged SI ordinate. Mineral-specific counts are labelled. The equal-width display does not imply equal sample sizes; compare the counts and raw observations alongside the smoothed shape.

9.13.10 Counts or percentages of saturation states

Applies to: Mineral saturation distribution.

For each mineral the stacked bar contains the counts of negative, exactly zero and positive finite SI values. Percentage mode divides each count by that mineral's own assessed total; count mode keeps integer observations. Unassessed records do not contribute to any of the three classes. A bar dominated by supersaturated observations indicates frequent positive SI under the specified database and solution assumptions, not frequent observed precipitation events.

9.13.11 Paired mineral indices or SI versus a measurement

Applies to: SI scatter plot.

9.13.13 Coordinates used for distances

Applies to: SI interpolated map.

Geographic location and distance are separate operations. Projected input uses its source distance units. For WGS84 longitude / latitude → local metres, AquaProv converts degrees to a local metric approximation about the mean longitude λ₀ and latitude φ₀:

x = R(λ − λ₀)cos φ₀;   y = R(φ − φ₀)
R = 6,371,008.8 m; angles in radians
dᵢⱼ = √[(xᵢ − xⱼ)² + (yᵢ − yⱼ)²] (9.5)

The local approximation is restricted to extents no greater than 3° in either direction and latitudes within ±85°. For larger or more precise studies, choose a suitable projected CRS and consistent distance units. Changing the basemap for display does not change the analysis distance coordinates. Duplicate locations and fewer than three non-collinear observations cannot define these interpolated surfaces without deliberate data preparation.

The current Distance coordinates control also offers automatic UTM/UPS and a custom projected CRS in metres. These transform WGS84 positions through the selected projection before calculating distances; they do not use the local equation above. Enter Analysis CRS for the custom mode, and retain the selected CRS in the calculation record. For existing projected X/Y, Source CRS override declares the original projected system.

Choose Grid definition → Cells per axis for 20–1,000 cells per axis, or Cell size in analysis units for explicit spacing in the analysis coordinate units. The cell-size option has a 1,000,000-point limit. Resolution changes where predictions are evaluated; it does not increase sample support. Colour classification and Surface drawing change the presentation. Retain exported class boundaries when comparing Equal intervals, Quantiles, Natural Breaks (Jenks) or custom classes; large-grid Natural Breaks uses a bounded histogram approximation.

9.13.14 Inverse-distance weighting (IDW)

Applies to: SI interpolated map.

At target s₀, IDW calculates a normalized weighted average of the selected neighbouring sample values vᵢ. Nearest neighbours → All samples includes all eligible observations; a positive count selects the nearest available observations and includes ties at the cutoff distance. With distance dᵢ = ‖s₀ − sᵢ‖ and positive power p, the sums run over that selected set (Shepard, 1968):

wᵢ = dᵢ−p / ∑ⱼdⱼ−p
v̂(s₀) = ∑ᵢwᵢvᵢ (9.6)

If the target exactly matches a sample, its observed value is returned. Internally distances are normalized by the nearest distance before exponentiation for numerical stability; normalized weights are unchanged. All weights are nonnegative and sum to 1, so predictions stay within the sample value range. Increasing p favours nearby samples more strongly. At distances 1 and 2 with p = 2, illustrative values 10 and 30 yield (10 + 30/4)/(1 + 1/4) = 14.

The grid is restricted to the convex hull, and to the study-boundary intersection when selected, unless boundary extrapolation is explicitly enabled. Grid resolution changes evaluation density, not the number of observations. IDW supplies no prediction variance or probability. Boundaries clip the display; they are not physical groundwater-flow barriers.

9.13.15 Ordinary kriging: model, weights and variance

Applies to: SI interpolated map.

Ordinary kriging assumes an unknown locally constant mean and an isotropic covariance depending only on distance. Let a be range, s the structured partial sill, and n₀ the nugget, all in consistent distance or squared measurement units. With r = d/a (Cressie, 1993):

Spherical C(d) = s(1 − 1.5r + 0.5r³) for r < 1; otherwise 0
Exponential C(d) = s exp(−3r) (9.7)

For the exponential model, a is the practical range where structured covariance is s exp(−3), about 5% of s. Form Kᵢⱼ = C(dᵢⱼ) + n₀δᵢⱼ, cᵢ = C(‖sᵢ−s₀‖), and the ones vector 1. The unscaled equivalent of AquaProv's linear system is:

K w + 1μ = c;   1ᵀw = 1
v̂(s₀) = wᵀv
σ²pred(s₀) = s + n₀ − wᵀc − μ (9.8)

μ is the Lagrange multiplier under this covariance sign convention. The implementation scales covariances by s+n₀ to improve numerical conditioning. The sum-to-one constraint preserves a constant mean. Weights may be negative, so predictions need not stay within observed values. The reported variance is for a new observation, including the nugget; with a nonzero nugget, a coincident target is not forced to reproduce an observed sample exactly.

The Maps module uses user-supplied range, partial sill and nugget and does not fit a variogram automatically. Fitted models are provided separately in Assessment. Neither module silently estimates anisotropy or removes a trend. Validate the model with held-out data before using its interpolated pattern scientifically.

9.13.16 Interpolate a logarithmic saturation index

Applies to: SI interpolated map.

The selected mineral's finite sample SI values are passed to IDW or ordinary kriging after coordinate preparation. The worked figure uses Calcite, IDW power 2 and a 120 × 120 grid covering the full supplied Sukinda KML, with extrapolated cells flagged in the export. Geographic labels, true north and a geodesic scale accompany the map. Interpolation acts directly on SI, not on free-ion activities, concentration inputs or mineral abundance.

For IDW weights wᵢ, this has a useful logarithmic interpretation:

SÎ = ∑ᵢwᵢSIᵢ
10SÎ = ∏ᵢ(10SIᵢ)wᵢ (9.9)

Thus IDW averages log saturation ratios, equivalent to a weighted geometric mean of the sample ratios. It does not speciate a physically mixed water, and it is not generally equal to calculating SI after interpolating chemistry. Grid classification uses the sign of predicted SI; the SI = 0 contour marks an interpolated threshold, not a demonstrated reaction front. All interpretation remains conditional on data coverage, the interpolation method and original SI assumptions.

10.2 Projects and portability

Home and Projects offer search, sorting, and resume actions. A project remembers its last-used module. Missing or moved files can be reopened from their new location.

Projects embed the current dataset, so reopening does not require the original input file. Retain the original workbook separately for provenance. Imported study-area boundaries are also embedded. Switching modules preserves live edits; opening a different project or closing the application prompts about unsaved changes.

10.3 Delete a project deliberately

Deletion can remove the project folder

The Projects bin button identifies the exact target before deletion. A dedicated project folder can be removed with its datasets, settings, exports, and other contents. For a project saved in a shared or unrecognized folder, only the .aquaprov document is removed. Original inputs and exports outside the target stay in place. Read the confirmation carefully.

10.4 Writing a scientific methods and results account

A scientific account should connect each reported result to its observations, calculation and evidential limits. Reproducible environmental modelling requires a traceable relationship between data, configuration and outputs (White et al., 2020). Name AquaProv and its version, then cite the scientific methods actually used. A software citation does not replace the method or dataset citation.

10.4.1 Example methods paragraph: hydrochemistry and statistics

The following is an editable writing model, not a claim about a new analysis: “Groundwater chemistry was analysed using AquaProv V. 1.0. The dataset comprised [number] sampling events from [number] wells during [period], obtained from [data source]. Major-ion concentrations were converted from [input units] to meq/L and screened using [charge-balance convention and tolerance]. Relative composition was examined using Piper diagrams (Piper, 1944). PCA used [variables], [transformation], [centering/standardization] and [missing-data policy], retaining [number] components that explained [percentage] of the analysed variance (Jolliffe & Cadima, 2016).” Replace every bracketed item with the recorded setting or result.

10.4.2 Example methods paragraph: spatial prediction and uncertainty

“Coordinates were supplied in [CRS] and transformed to [distance system] for interpolation. [IDW/ordinary kriging] used [power or covariance model and range, partial sill and nugget], with [boundary and extrapolation policy] (Shepard, 1968; Cressie, 1993). Predictive performance was evaluated using [holdout design and metrics]. Measurement propagation used [fixed/relative-SD/laboratory-SD mode], [censoring treatment], [dependence assumption], [draw count] and seed [value]. Reported probabilities are conditional on those assumptions, and scenario sensitivity was examined using [alternatives] (Pianosi et al., 2016).” Cite only the interpolation method actually applied.

10.4.3 Example methods paragraph: indices and mineral saturation

“The [WQI/DWQI/CCME] calculation used [parameter set], [objective sources and editions], [rating functions], [weights where applicable] and [classification boundaries]. [Number] of [number] records met the stated completeness rule. Mineral saturation indices were calculated by initial aqueous speciation with [IPhreeqc version], [database and hash], [temperature], [density], [pH and alkalinity/carbon basis] and [redox specification] (Charlton & Parkhurst, 2011; Parkhurst & Appelo, 2013). Unassessed mineral–sample combinations and charge-balance review flags were retained in the exported audit.” Add the corresponding index-method citation from Chapter 8 once the chosen method is known.

10.4.4 Separating observations, results and interpretation

In the Results section, state what was observed or calculated before suggesting why: give the retained count, measurement range, explained variance, prediction error or conditional probability, then describe the plotted pattern. In the Discussion, compare that pattern with the site geology, sampling design and published evidence. Avoid replacing a quantitative statement with a colour description or assigning a source solely from a PCA loading or diagram field. Label synthetic arithmetic checks in this manual as illustrative if reused.

Every figure caption should identify the dataset or subset, the quantity and units, the sample count and the meaning of colour or uncertainty intervals. A map caption should also describe the interpolation and unsampled-area policy; an SI caption should identify the database and relevant solution assumptions. Keep complete settings and numerical tables with the figure so readers can distinguish a measured observation from a modelled or interpolated value.

Chapter 11: A worked study and troubleshooting

The Sukinda example links the individual modules into a single sequence. Its purpose is to show how the same source table can be audited, described and mapped while preserving the assumptions attached to each step. It does not establish a validated field interpretation or a regulatory assessment of the study area.

11.1 The study input

The Sukinda worked data are attributed to their associated source publication (Sahoo et al., 2025). Use Sukinda_CGWB_Data_2024.xlsx, worksheet Selected_47_Samples. The table contains 47 records with major ions, pH, EC, TDS, location, well-type labels, and selected trace metals. The local filename provides a source attribution; the primary report details still need verification before reuse in a manuscript.

This tutorial demonstrates software behavior. It does not establish a site assessment, contamination source, or drinking-water compliance conclusion. Download the workbook and inspect the interactive local data table.

11.2 Audit measurement meaning

  1. Create a project named Sukinda tutorial and import the workbook.
  2. Review the normalized chemistry headings and the 47-row count. Preserve the original file.
  3. Open Assessment and inspect the suggested schema. Major ions and TDS are mg/L, EC is µS/cm, pH is unitless, and trace-metal headings are ppb; confirm these against the source before analysis.
  4. Generate Measurement quality & charge balance. Record flagged or unassessable rows rather than removing them automatically.

CO3 is zero throughout this worksheet. Some SO4 measurements are also zero. Keep reported zeros; they limit log-scale and log-ratio methods.

11.3 Describe the major-ion composition

Generate Triangle Piper using mg/L inputs. It internally converts ions to meq/L and normalizes cations and anions separately. Compare group labels without treating them as contamination classes.

11.4 Inspect relationships and components

Switch to Statistics. Select pH, Ca, Mg, Na, K, HCO3, Cl, SO4. Generate Pearson correlation, then PCA with sample-SD standardization, three retained components, and no Varimax rotation. Review the scree plot and explain the retained count before using it in a report.

All 47 rows are complete for these eight variables. CO3 is excluded because it is constant; TDS, hardness, and alkalinity are excluded from this particular model to avoid adding totals alongside their contributors. This selection is for a worked example, not a claim that it is uniquely optimal.

11.5 Map measurements before interpolating

Open Maps and set X to Longitude (°E), Y to Latitude (°N), and source CRS to WGS84. Select TDS for point color. Start with the plain coordinate map so that measured sites and gradients remain visible without a network background.

Import assets/data/Sukinda_CGWB_Data_Study_Area.kml under Study-area boundary and enable Use study boundary. All 47 samples are inside this polygon. For IDW choose WGS84 → local metres, power 2, resolution 100, and Use only samples inside boundary. The complete-boundary example enables Fill entire boundary (extrapolate); its hatched areas extend beyond the sample hull. Turn that option off to reproduce the restricted-coverage comparison. Export the measurement map and interpolation separately.

11.8 Developing an evidence-based discussion of the worked study

The Sukinda sequence begins with measurements associated with the aquifer-management material of Sahoo et al. (2025). Use the import audit to establish which observations support each subsequent result. A facies diagram and a PCA plot may summarize related major-ion contrasts, but that agreement is not independent confirmation of a contamination source because both views reuse the same measurements (Güler et al., 2002).

A defensible discussion would report the actual chemical pattern, compare it with local hydrogeological evidence, and examine whether it persists under documented changes in cohort, preprocessing or uncertainty assumptions. A spatial surface should be discussed alongside sample coverage and held-out predictive performance. An index category should be accompanied by the individual-objective results, and a mineral SI interpretation should retain the solution audit. This chain of evidence supports a transparent account without turning the tutorial into an unverified field conclusion.

11.9 Extending the study to indices and saturation

Use the prepared index CSV and the original chemistry workbook for their respective workflows. In WQI & DWQI, document the objective profile, rating rules, weights and incomplete samples. In Mineral SI, document the PHREEQC database, temperature, density, alkalinity basis and mineral-specific missing results. Return to the original measurements whenever an output appears inconsistent; different modules can legitimately use different eligible sample sets.

11.10 When the result is unexpected

First identify whether the problem concerns import, calculation, presentation or export. Confirm the active project, module, selected plot and dataset. Regenerate if a method setting has changed. An old figure can remain visible until generation; the exported figure represents the last rendered state.

11.11 Import and chemistry

Several columns map to the same name.

Two headings normalize to the same AquaProv field. Review their units and species, then retain one verified measurement column for that field. Do not silently discard one of two different analyses.

The plot requires CO3, but my dataset has no carbonate column.

Piper and several related diagrams require the eight measured ion fields. Missing carbonate is not automatically zero. Choose another supported workflow or obtain a scientifically justified source value.

My sample IDs lost leading zeros.

Use text cells for IDs in Excel. A number displayed with leading zeros remains a number internally. CSV identifier-like columns preserve strings on import, but cannot restore zeros already lost upstream.

Text such as n.d. or <0.01 prevents analysis.

Keep the qualifier, reporting limit, and laboratory meaning. Assessment can model explicit censoring; ordinary statistics cannot interpret an undefined non-detect value.

Schoeller warns about a non-positive log limit.

The supplied Sukinda worksheet includes zero sulfate. A logarithmic axis cannot show zero. Do not replace measured zeros solely to make the warning disappear; report exclusions or use a defensible alternative display.

11.12 Statistics and surfaces

PCA rejects my retained component count.

The number cannot exceed the numerical rank of the selected complete data. Remove constant/redundant variables deliberately and choose a supported count.

A 3D loading plot looks different after rotation.

Camera rotation changes the projection of the same coordinates. It does not recompute PCA. Use orthographic plane views to compare axes; signs can differ between component conventions.

Interpolation rejects duplicate locations.

Select a single campaign or define an aggregation rule supported by your sampling design. Distinct time observations at one well cannot automatically be treated as different spatial sites.

A surface cannot be built from my points.

At least three unique, non-collinear X/Y sites and finite numeric Z values are needed. A transect of collinear samples does not define a two-dimensional surface.

11.13 Maps and exports

The map appears in the wrong place.

Check X/Y order, decimal degrees versus projected metres, datum, and UTM zone/hemisphere. A plausible-looking map does not verify the coordinate system.

The basemap is incomplete or unavailable.

Use the status message and Retry background control. Network failure can leave correctly positioned markers visible. Plain coordinates works without online tiles.

The contour fills an unsampled part of my boundary.

Review Fill entire boundary (extrapolate). It starts checked when using the boundary controls; disable it to restrict coverage to the sampled area. Check flagged extrapolated cells in results.

My export does not reflect the latest method changes.

Generate after applying analysis settings. Export saves the last rendered figure, including its current camera.

The exported page dimensions differ from my settings.

Tight cropping trims the page. Disable it when exact dimensions are required; then verify the saved file.

My export failed or was only partially saved.

Read the destination and error message, check file locks, available disk space, and file names. A failed batch may identify files already saved; do not treat it as complete.

11.14 Report a reproducible issue

Record the Python version, selected module/plot, exact steps, error text, input columns and units, and a minimal anonymized dataset where possible. Contact the AquaProv authors. The in-app issue and repository cards become active only when real URLs are configured.

11.15 Additional checks for index and SI workflows

An index is Unassessed. Review every selected parameter, its numeric objective and the sample's missing values. Arithmetic weights are not silently redistributed around a missing chemical. For CCME, check the number of variables and distinct measured occasions in each site/period group.

A mineral is unassessed. Check the dissolved components required for that mineral, the phase name in the selected database and the redox specification where relevant. Missing chemistry and undefined SI are not numerical zero. Review the solution audit before changing the plot.

An interpolated surface is unavailable. Check complete, distinct, non-collinear sites, consistent coordinates and units. Resolve repeated observations at the same location deliberately. Changing the grid resolution cannot create spatial information that is absent from the sample set.
